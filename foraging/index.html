Nuts, Fruits & Wild Edibles

How to find, identify, pick and process wild nuts and fruit in the temperate north-east US (New Jersey, USDA zones 6–7) and the Mediterranean (Algarve, Portugal). Conservative by design: when in doubt, leave it.

If you are not 100% sure of the identification, do not eat it
  • Confirm every plant with a regional field guide and, ideally, a local expert. Check several features (leaf, bark, flower, fruit, seed, habitat), not just one. This app is a reference, not a positive ID.
  • Never eat white berries, or any berry you cannot name.
  • Never eat wild mushrooms based on this page. Fungi are out of scope.
  • Even with a known, safe food, try a small amount first and wait several hours before eating more.
  • Allergies: anyone allergic to one tree nut may react to others. Chestnut and fig can cross-react with latex allergy. Know who in your group has allergies and where their epinephrine auto-injector is.
  • Don't pick near roads, rail lines, power-line corridors, sprayed fields, orchards or lawns, golf courses, industrial sites, dumps or polluted water. Pick above knee height (dogs).
Suspected poisoning

Stop eating. Keep a sample. Call US Poison Control 1-800-222-1222 · Portugal CIAV 800 250 250. Collapse, seizures or trouble breathing: 911 (US) / 112 (Portugal/EU). Don't make anyone vomit unless told to. Full steps

Reference only. Written descriptions can't replace hands-on identification by an expert. Plants vary by region and season, and people vary in how they react. Get permission on private land and follow local rules. Children, pregnant or breastfeeding people and anyone on medication: be extra cautious.

Nuts

Most nuts are ripe when they fall on their own. These rules apply to every nut:

  • Collect often, daily if you can. Squirrels, mould and insects take nuts left on the ground.
  • Discard nuts with small round holes (weevil exits), cracks or mould, and any that feel light for their size.
  • Float test (acorns, hulled walnuts, hickory, chestnuts, hazelnuts): tip them into a bucket of water. Floaters are usually empty or wormy, so throw them out. Sinkers are usually good. The test isn't perfect, so still check every kernel.
  • Mouldy, shrivelled or discoloured kernels can carry aflatoxin, a liver toxin that cooking doesn't destroy. When in doubt, throw it out.
  • Kill hidden larvae: freeze nuts for at least 48 hours at 0 °F (−18 °C) before storing.
Acorns (oaks) Quercus spp. · NE US Sep–Nov · Algarve Oct–Jan

Leach out tannins first

Identify
A nut sitting in a scaly cup (cap). Three groups:
  • White oak group (NE US): leaves with rounded lobes and no bristle tips. Acorns ripen in one season and are lower in tannin (milder). They often sprout soon after falling, so process them promptly.
  • Red oak group (NE US): leaf lobes pointed, with bristle tips. Acorns take two seasons to mature and are higher in tannin, so they are more bitter and need longer leaching. They keep better before processing.
  • Iberia: holm oak (azinheira) and cork oak (sobreiro) are evergreen with small, leathery leaves. Cork oak has thick, spongy, corky bark. Holm oak bears the mildest acorns, and some trees' acorns need only brief leaching. Taste-test every tree. Cork oak acorns are usually more bitter.
Where
NE US: forests, parks, old fields, everywhere. Algarve: inland hills and cork-oak woodland (Serra do Caldeirão, Monchique).
When
NE US: Sep–Nov, peak Oct. Algarve: Oct–Jan. Ripe acorns are brown or tan, and the cap twists off easily. Heavy crops ("mast years") come every 2–5 years.
Collect
Pick them up from the ground daily. Skip green, cracked, mouldy or holed acorns. Float test, then discard the floaters. Spread them out to dry the same day, because damp piles mould fast.
Shell
Dry for a few days until the shells are brittle, then crack with a hammer, rock or nutcracker and peel. Rub off as much of the thin brown inner skin as you can, because it is very bitter. Discard dark, mushy or wormy kernels.

Leach the tannins: method 1, cold water

Slow. Keeps the starch intact, so the flour binds for bread and pancakes.

  1. Grind or chop fresh shelled kernels coarsely. A blender with water works. Smaller pieces leach faster.
  2. Put the meal in a large jar or bucket and cover it with 3–4 times its volume of cold, clean water. Stir.
  3. Change the water 1–2 times a day: let it settle, pour off the brown water and refill. You can also hang the meal in a cloth bag and change the water around it.
  4. Keep going until it tastes bland. This usually takes several days to two weeks, longest for red oak acorns.
  5. Do the taste test (below). Then strain through a cloth and squeeze dry.

Leach the tannins: method 2, hot water

Fast, but it cooks the starch, so the flour won't bind well. Best for whole or chunked acorns that you will roast or add to stews.

  1. Put shelled kernels (whole or halved) in a pot of water and bring to a boil. Keep a second pot of water heating.
  2. When the water turns tea-brown, drain and move the acorns straight into the second pot of already-hot water. Refill and reheat the first pot.
  3. Repeat. Expect many changes (often 5–10 or more) over 1–3 hours. Red oak acorns need more.
  4. Stop when the water stays nearly clear and a piece passes the taste test.
Taste test for bitterness

Chew a small piece for a few seconds, then spit it out. If it tastes bitter, or your mouth feels dry and puckered, tannins remain, so keep leaching. Fully leached acorns taste bland to mildly nutty. Unleached acorns can upset the stomach, and large amounts of tannin may harm the kidneys.

Flour
Spread the leached, squeezed meal thinly on a tray. Dry it in the sun, near (not over) a stove, or in an oven on its lowest setting with the door propped open. Stir often until it is completely dry and crumbly. Grind fine, sieve, and re-grind the coarse part. It has no gluten, so mix it with wheat flour for bread, or use it in pancakes, flatbread or to thicken stews.
Store
Whole dried acorns in the shell keep for several months somewhere cool, dry, airy and rodent-proof. Leached wet meal keeps 2–3 days in the fridge, or freeze it. Keep dry flour airtight, in the freezer if possible, because acorns contain oil that goes rancid.
Black walnut Juglans nigra · Sep–Nov (NE US)

Husk stains: wear gloves Very hard shell

Identify
Large tree. Leaves are 1–2 ft (30–60 cm) long and compound, with 15–23 narrow, toothed leaflets. The end leaflet is often small or missing. Bark is dark and deeply furrowed in a diamond pattern. The fruit is a round green husk, 1.5–2.5 in (4–6 cm), with a strong citrus-spicy smell when scratched. Inside is a deeply ridged, black-brown, very hard nut. Cut a twig lengthwise and the pith is chambered, like a ladder.
Look-alikes
Tree-of-heaven (Ailanthus) has similar leaves, but they smell rank, each leaflet has a gland-tipped tooth near its base, and it makes papery winged seeds, not nuts. Hickories have fewer leaflets (5–9) and husks that split into 4. Butternut (rare) has oblong, sticky husks and is also edible.
Where
NE US: field edges, river bottoms, fence rows, parks. The English (Persian) walnut, Juglans regia, grows in northern Portugal. Process it the same way, but its husk splits open by itself and its shell is thin.
When
Sep–Oct, into Nov. The nut is ripe when the husk turns yellow-green and dents under thumb pressure. Nuts fall on their own.
Collect
Gather from the ground within a few days of falling, before the husks turn black and mushy, which darkens the kernel and makes it harsh. Or shake branches over a tarp. Falling walnuts are heavy: wear a hat and eye protection, and never stand under the branch you are shaking.

Hull

Wear waterproof gloves (rubber or nitrile), old clothes and eye protection. The juice stains skin brown for 1–2 weeks and can irritate it.

  1. Split the husks by rolling each nut under your boot on concrete or a hard board, or stomp on them. Some people spread them on a gravel drive and drive over them.
  2. Pull or scrape off the husk. Small white maggots in the husk are walnut husk fly larvae. They don't harm the nut.
  3. Scrub the nuts in a bucket of water with a stiff brush. Discard floaters, which are usually empty.
  4. Dump husks away from vegetable gardens, because walnut husks and roots release juglone, which harms tomatoes and many other plants.
Cure
Spread the nuts 1–2 layers deep on screens or in mesh bags in a cool, dry, airy, shaded place, out of reach of squirrels, for 2–3 weeks. They are cured when a kernel snaps crisply instead of bending.
Crack
Stand the nut on its point on a hard surface (anvil, block or flat rock) and strike the top with a hammer. Or squeeze it in a bench vise until it cracks, then rotate and repeat. Wear eye protection, because shell fragments fly. Some people soak the nuts in hot water for an hour or two first so the kernels come out in bigger pieces. Pick out the meat with a nutpick or a nail. Expect a small yield for the effort.
Store
Cured nuts in the shell keep up to about a year somewhere cool, dry and rodent-proof. Kernels keep for months airtight in the fridge, or 1–2 years in the freezer. Discard kernels that smell of paint, taste harsh (rancid) or look mouldy.
Hickory nuts Carya ovata (shagbark), C. laciniosa (shellbark) · Sep–Nov (NE US)

Bitternut hickory: too bitter to eat

Identify
Shagbark: grey bark peeling in long, loose, vertical strips. Compound leaves, usually with 5 leaflets (sometimes 7), the end three the largest. Shellbark: similar shaggy bark, usually 7 leaflets and larger nuts; grows in bottomlands. The husk is thick, green turning brown, and splits into 4 parts all the way to the base when ripe. The nut is pale tan, 4-ridged and hard-shelled, with a sweet kernel.
Avoid
Bitternut hickory (Carya cordiformis) has bright sulphur-yellow buds, 7–11 slender leaflets, fairly smooth bark and a thin husk with 4 ridges on its upper half. The kernel is intensely bitter and not worth eating. Pignut and mockernut hickory are edible but variable or tiny. Taste a crumb of any hickory and spit out a bitter one.
Where
NE US: upland and bottomland forest, field edges. Not native to Europe.
When
Sep–Nov, peak Oct. Husks open on the tree and the nuts fall.
Collect
Gather from the ground often, or shake over a tarp. Discard nuts with weevil holes, and float-test to remove the floaters.
Cure
Dry in the shell for 1–2 weeks somewhere airy, until the kernel snaps.
Crack
Lay the nut on its side on a hard surface and strike the edge (the seam), not the point, with a hammer. A vise also works. The kernel sits in chambers, so break the shell into quarters and pick out the meat.
Shortcut: hickory "milk"
This is a traditional Native American method. Pound whole nuts, shell and all, into coarse bits. Simmer them in water, then let it settle: most of the shell sinks. Ladle off the rich, nutty liquid and strain it through a cloth. Use it in porridge, soup or as a drink.
Store
As for black walnut.
Chestnuts Castanea mollissima, C. sativa, C. dentata · NE US Sep–Oct · Portugal Oct–Nov

Toxic look-alike: horse chestnut / buckeye Cook before eating

Which chestnuts
American chestnut (C. dentata) was nearly wiped out by chestnut blight in the early 1900s. It survives mostly as root sprouts that rarely bear. In the NE US the usual source is planted Chinese chestnut (C. mollissima) in yards, parks and old orchards. In Portugal it is the European sweet chestnut (C. sativa, castanheiro), common in the north and centre. In the Algarve it grows mainly in the Serra de Monchique.
Identify
Leaves are simple (one blade per stalk), long and narrow, usually 5–10 in (12–25 cm). Straight parallel veins each end in a sharp, bristle-tipped tooth. Flowers are long, creamy catkins in early summer, with a strong smell. The bur (husk) is round and densely covered in long, sharp, needle-like spines, and splits into 2–4 sections. Nuts: usually 2–3 per bur. They are glossy brown, flattened on one or two sides, with a pointed tip topped by a small tassel and a pale, rough base.
When
NE US (Chinese chestnut): mid-Sep–Oct. Portugal: Oct–Nov. São Martinho (11 Nov) is chestnut-roasting season.
Collect
Gather fallen nuts and burs every day or two, because chestnuts dry out, mould and get weevils quickly. Wear thick leather gloves: spines go through cloth. Roll a bur under your boot to open it, then pick the nuts out with gloves or tongs. Skip flat, empty nuts and any with holes. Float-test and discard the floaters.
Store
Unlike most nuts, chestnuts are moist and starchy and spoil like fruit. Keep them in a paper or perforated bag in the fridge (32–40 °F / 0–4 °C) and use within a few weeks. For longer storage, cook, peel and freeze them. Peeled chestnuts dried until rock-hard keep for months and can be ground into flour.

Score and cook

  1. Score every nut. Cut an X or a slit through the shell on the rounded side with a sharp knife. Unscored chestnuts can burst violently when heated.
  2. Roast in an oven at 400–425 °F (200–220 °C) for 15–25 min, or in a perforated pan over a fire, shaking often. They are done when the shells curl back and the flesh is tender. Or boil them for 15–20 min.
  3. Wrap them in a towel for 5 minutes, then peel while warm. Remove the shell and the bitter brown inner skin.

Raw chestnuts are astringent and hard to digest, so cook them.

Sweet chestnut vs horse chestnut

Horse chestnut and buckeyes are toxic

Horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum) is a common park and street tree in Europe and the US, and native US buckeyes are the same genus. Eating them causes vomiting and diarrhoea, and larger amounts cause muscle twitching, weakness and collapse. Children are most at risk. They are not edible raw, roasted or boiled. Never taste-test to tell the two apart.

Hazelnut / filbert Corylus americana, C. avellana · Aug–Sep

Pick early or the squirrels win

Identify
A multi-stemmed shrub, 3–10 ft (1–3 m); European hazel can grow taller. Leaves are simple, roundish-oval, double-toothed, slightly heart-shaped at the base and soft-hairy. Long dangling male catkins appear in late winter. Nuts grow in clusters of 2–6, each wrapped in a leafy husk with a ragged edge. Beaked hazel (C. cornuta) has a long tubular husk with irritating bristles, so wear gloves.
Where
NE US: American hazel at woodland edges, hedgerows and clearings. Iberia: European hazel (aveleira) in damp mountain valleys of northern Portugal and Spain. Uncommon in the Algarve.
When
Aug–Sep. Pick when the husks start to brown at the edges and the nut inside has turned tan to brown, even if the husk is still mostly green. Squirrels, chipmunks and jays usually strip the bushes before the nuts fall.
Husk and dry
Spread the clusters somewhere warm, dry and airy for 1–2 weeks, until the husks dry and loosen, then rub them off (gloves on). Float-test and discard the floaters (empty "blanks"). Dry the nuts in the shell for another 2–3 weeks, until the kernel is crisp.
Crack
Use a nutcracker or pliers, or tap lightly with a hammer or stone on a hard surface. Eat raw, or toast lightly and rub off the skins.
Store
In the shell, kept dry and cool, they last several months. Store shelled nuts in the fridge or freezer.
Pecan Carya illinoinensis · Oct–Nov

NE US: planted trees only

Identify
A tall hickory. Compound leaves have 9–17 narrow leaflets, often slightly sickle-curved. Bark is grey with scaly ridges. The husk is thin with 4 ridges and splits into 4 when ripe. The nut is smooth, oblong and thin-shelled, brown with dark streaks.
Where
Native to the south-central US (Mississippi valley, Texas). In New Jersey only planted trees grow, and they often bear poorly or produce partly filled nuts.
Collect
Oct–Nov, after the husks split. Gather from the ground, or shake or pole the branches over a tarp. Discard nuts with weevil holes and any dark or shrivelled kernels.
Dry and crack
Dry in the shell for 1–2 weeks somewhere airy, until the kernel snaps. Squeeze two nuts together in your hand, or use a nutcracker. Remove the bitter papery partitions.
Store
Shelled nuts keep for months in the fridge, or a year or more in the freezer.
Beechnuts Fagus grandifolia, F. sylvatica · Sep–Oct

Only small amounts raw

Identify
Smooth, pale grey bark: the tree people carve initials into. Leaves are simple and oval, with straight parallel veins. They are sharply toothed on American beech and wavy-edged on European beech. Young trees often keep dead, papery tan leaves all winter. The husk is small, 4-parted and covered in soft prickles. It opens to release 2 small, 3-sided nuts with a thin, shiny brown shell.
Where
NE US: mature forest. Europe: forests of central and northern Europe; rare in southern Portugal.
When
Sep–Oct, often after the first frost. Good crops come only every few years. In off years most nuts are empty, so squeeze or cut a few to check.
Collect
Shake over a tarp, or pick them out of the leaf litter. They are small and slow to gather.
Process
Peel the thin shell with a thumbnail or knife. Raw beechnuts in quantity can upset the stomach, so eat only a handful raw. Roast them in a dry pan until fragrant before eating more. They are oily, so store shelled nuts cold.
Pine nuts Pinus pinea (stone pine), P. edulis & P. monophylla (pinyon) · cones Dec–Mar (Portugal) · Sep–Oct (US West)

Never confuse with yew

Identify
Stone pine (pinheiro-manso) has a flat-topped, umbrella-shaped crown, long needles in pairs and reddish, plated bark. Its cones are large (3–6 in / 8–15 cm), heavy, roundish and glossy brown, and take about 3 years to mature. The seeds are about ¾ in (2 cm) long, with a hard, dark shell coated in black powder. Pinyon (US Southwest and Great Basin) is a small, bushy pine with short needles and small cones holding large, wingless seeds.
Where
Stone pine is very common across Portugal, including sandy coastal areas and the inland Algarve. Pinyon grows in the dry hills of the US West, not the NE.
Other pines
The seeds of nearly all true pines are edible, but most species' seeds (e.g. the NE's white and pitch pine) are too tiny to be worth the effort. To confirm a true pine, look for needles in bundles with a papery sheath at the base, and woody cones.
Yew warning
Yew (Taxus) is not a pine. It has flat, soft, dark-green needles in two rows, no woody cones, and single seeds sitting in a red, fleshy, open cup. The seed and foliage are deadly: a small amount can stop the heart.
Collect (stone pine)
Mature cones are brown and closed. Gather them in winter (Dec–Mar) from the tree or the ground. In summer heat, cones left on the tree open and drop their seeds, which you can pick up underneath. In Portugal stone pine cones are a valuable, regulated crop that belongs to the landowner, so ask first.
Open the cones
Spread the cones in full summer sun for a few weeks until the scales open. Or warm them near a fire or in a low oven until they open. Then shake or knock out the seeds. The resin is flammable, so never put cones in or directly over flames, and stay with them. For pinyon, pick the cones just before they open and dry them in the sun, or gather fallen seeds. Wear gloves, and remove sticky pitch from skin with cooking oil.
Crack
Stone pine shells are thick. Tap them with a hammer or between two flat stones, or use pliers. The black dust stains, so rinse or rub it off first. Peel off the thin papery skin if it is loose.
Store
In the shell, kept cool, dry and airtight, they last for months. Shelled pine nuts are very oily and go rancid fast, so keep them in the fridge or freezer.

Fruits & berries

Good practice for all fruit
  • Ripe fruit comes away easily. If you have to tug, it isn't ready.
  • Pick into shallow, rigid containers. Soft berries crush under their own weight, so keep layers to about 4 in (10 cm).
  • Keep fruit shaded and cool. Refrigerate it unwashed, and wash just before eating or processing.
  • Eat only ripe fruit. Many edible fruits are harmful unripe, and many seeds and pits are toxic.
Blackberry Rubus spp. (NE: R. allegheniensis; Iberia: R. ulmifolius, silva) · NE Jul–Sep · Algarve Jun–Sep

Among the safest groups Thorns, ticks

Identify
Thorny canes that arch or trail. Leaves are compound, with 3–5 toothed leaflets. Flowers are white or pink with 5 petals. The fruit is a cluster of many small juicy beads (an aggregate). The white core stays inside the berry when you pick it; raspberries come off hollow. Fruit grows on second-year canes.
Look-alikes
Other Rubus (dewberry, raspberry) are edible. No dangerous plant has the same aggregate fruit on thorny canes, but still confirm the plant. Mulberry fruit looks similar but grows on a tree.
Ripe
Fully black (red berries are unripe and sour), plump, slightly dull rather than shiny, and drops into your hand with a gentle pull.
Harvest
Wear long sleeves, trousers and leather-palmed gloves, or at least one gloved hand to hold the canes. Draw canes toward you with a hooked stick. Pick above knee height. Check for ticks afterwards (NE US: Lyme disease). Watch for wasps, and for snakes in dense brambles.
Keep
1–2 days in the fridge. To freeze, spread them on a tray, freeze, then bag. Use for jam, jelly, syrup or fruit leather (seedy). Skip mouldy or fermented fruit.
Raspberries & wineberry Rubus idaeus, R. occidentalis, R. phoenicolasius · Jun–Aug (NE US)

Among the safest groups

Identify
Prickly canes. Compound leaves (3–5 leaflets), often whitish underneath. The fruit comes off its core hollow, like a thimble.
  • Black raspberry: arching canes with a waxy blue-white coating that rubs off; black fruit (Jun–Jul).
  • Red raspberry: red fruit (Jul–Aug).
  • Wineberry (invasive, common in NJ): stems covered in red bristly hairs; bright orange-red, glossy fruit (Jul).
Ripe
Full colour, and slips off the core with almost no pull.
Harvest
Very fragile, so use small, shallow containers. Same gear and tick precautions as for blackberry. Not common wild in the Algarve.
Keep
Eat within a day, or freeze on a tray. Use for jam or syrup, or dry them.
Mulberry Morus rubra, M. alba, M. nigra · NE Jun–Jul · Algarve May–Aug

Ripe fruit only

Identify
A tree, not a bramble. Leaves are toothed and very variable on the same tree: unlobed and heart-shaped, mitten-shaped, or with several lobes. Broken leaf stalks ooze milky sap. The fruit looks like an elongated blackberry, ½–1¼ in (1–3 cm) long, on a short stalk.
Where
NE US: red (native) and white (introduced) mulberry on streets, fence lines, woods edges and vacant lots. Portugal: black mulberry (amoreira) in villages, farmyards and gardens.
Ripe
Dark purple to black (or deep red-black), soft and juicy; it falls at a touch. Unripe fruit (green, red or hard) and the milky sap can upset the stomach. Some white mulberry trees bear white fruit. Beginners should skip them: the no-white-berries rule has no exceptions on this page.
Harvest
Spread a tarp or old sheet under the branches and shake. Ripe fruit falls; unripe fruit stays. The juice stains hands and clothes purple.
Keep
Very perishable: eat or process within a day. Freeze them, dry them like raisins, or make jam (they are low in acid, so add lemon juice). The little green stalk is harmless; snip it off for jam.
Elderberry Sambucus canadensis, S. nigra · NE Aug–Sep · Iberia Jul–Sep

Must cook Deadly look-alikes Stems, leaves, seeds toxic

Never eat raw elderberries

The raw berries (especially unripe ones), seeds, stems, leaves, bark and roots contain cyanide-producing compounds (cyanogenic glycosides) that cause nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea. Use only the dark purple-black berries of Sambucus nigra / S. canadensis, completely destemmed, and cook them.

Identify
A woody shrub, 5–12 ft (1.5–4 m), with many stems. The bark has raised warty dots, and young stems have soft white pith inside. Leaves are opposite and compound, with 5–11 (usually 5–7) toothed leaflets. In early summer it has flat-topped, umbrella-like clusters of tiny cream-white flowers, 4–10 in (10–25 cm) across. The berries are about ¼ in (5 mm), purple-black, in large flat-topped clusters that droop when ripe.
Do NOT use
  • Red elderberry (S. racemosa): red berries in dome- or pyramid-shaped clusters. Ripens earlier in summer.
  • Dwarf elder / danewort (S. ebulus; Europe, including Iberia): not woody. It dies back to the ground each winter, grows 3–6 ft (1–2 m), holds its black berries in upright clusters and smells unpleasant. Toxic.
  • Pokeweed: purple-black berries in long, narrow, drooping spikes on smooth magenta stems, with large simple leaves. All parts are toxic.
  • Water hemlock, poison hemlock, hemlock water-dropwort (carrot family): also have white umbrella flower heads, but they are non-woody, with hollow stems and alternate, finely divided leaves. Deadly. Elder is a woody shrub with opposite leaves.
  • Devil's walking stick (Aralia spinosa, NE US): very spiny stems, huge compound leaves, black berries on pinkish stalks. Not a food.
Ripe
Every berry in the cluster is dark purple-black and soft, and the cluster has started to droop. Leave clusters that still have green or red berries.
Harvest
Snip whole clusters into a bucket with scissors or pruners. Birds compete, so check daily once the berries start to colour.

Destem, cook, strain

  1. Destem completely. Freeze the clusters on a tray for a few hours, then rub or shake the berries off, or strip them with a fork. Pick out every bit of stem and leaf, and every green or red berry.
  2. Rinse in a bowl of water and skim off debris and any berries that float.
  3. Cook: bring to a boil with a little water and simmer for at least 15 minutes, crushing the berries.
  4. For juice, syrup or jelly, strain out the seeds through a fine sieve or cloth.
  5. Keep syrup in the fridge. Can it only with a tested recipe (see the Food page).

The flowers (early summer) are also used for cordial and fritters. Shake out the insects and use only the flowers, no stems or leaves. Eat elderberry in moderation.

Wild grape Vitis spp. (riverbank, fox, frost, summer grape) · Aug–Oct (NE US)

Deadly look-alike: moonseed

Identify
A woody climbing vine with forked (branched) tendrils opposite the leaves. Leaves are simple, heart-shaped and often 3-lobed, with toothed edges. The leaf stalk joins at the leaf's edge, in the notch. Older vines have shreddy bark peeling in strips, and brown pith. Fruit hangs in clusters, blue-black to purple and often with a whitish bloom. Each berry has 1–4 pear-shaped seeds.
Where
NE US: woods edges, riversides, fences, hedgerows.
Ripe
Aug–Oct. Soft and fully coloured, with some sweetness under the tartness. Frost grape is best after a frost.
Check every vine
Before eating from any vine, cut several berries open. You must see 1–4 teardrop-shaped seeds. One flat, crescent-shaped seed means moonseed: discard everything from that vine. Also confirm tendrils and toothed leaves on the vine the cluster actually hangs from, because vines tangle together.
Harvest
Clip whole clusters with scissors or pruners. Lower high vines gently with a hooked stick instead of tearing them down.
Process
Crush and simmer the grapes, then strain through a cloth for juice or jelly. Wild grapes are tart, so add sugar. Wild grape juice can irritate skin and mouth because of tartrate crystals. Wear gloves for big batches, let the juice settle in the fridge for 1–2 days, then pour the clear juice off the gritty sediment. Young leaves (late spring) can be blanched and used as wraps.

Wild grape vs moonseed and other look-alikes

Moonseed can kill

Canada moonseed (Menispermum canadense) grows in the same places as wild grape and fruits at the same time, and its clusters look like grapes. Eating the fruit has caused deaths. Check every vine.

Identify
A small understory tree, usually growing in patches in rich, moist bottomland. American persimmon Diospyros virginiana · Sep–Dec, peak Oct–Nov (NE US)

Fully soft only

Identify
A tree with dark, thick bark broken into small, square, blocky plates, like alligator skin. Leaves are simple, oval, glossy and smooth-edged. The fruit is round, 1–2 in (2.5–5 cm), orange to purplish-orange, with a 4-lobed cap and several flat brown seeds.
Ripeness is everything
Unripe fruit is fiercely astringent: chalky and mouth-drying. Ripe fruit is very soft, wrinkled, almost translucent and mushy, and falls on its own or with a light shake. Ripening often follows a frost, but frost isn't required; softness is what counts. If it isn't squishy, wait.
Caution
Eating large amounts of unripe persimmon can form a hard mass (bezoar) in the stomach that may need endoscopy or surgery to remove. The risk is higher in people who have had stomach surgery. Eat only fully ripe fruit, in moderation.
Harvest
Collect from the ground daily, or shake the tree over a tarp. Handle gently.
Process
Press the fruit through a colander or food mill to remove skin, cap and seeds. Frozen pulp keeps for months. Use it in puddings and breads, or spread it thin and dry it as fruit leather.

No seeds or cores in quantity

A small tree, often with short spur shoots, sometimes thorny. Leaves are simple and toothed. It has pink or white apple blossom in spring. The fruit is a small apple, under 2 in (5 cm), red, yellow or green. It has a stem on top, the dried remains of the flower at the bottom, and a star-shaped seed core when cut across.

Fig Ficus carica · Jun–Oct, peak Aug–Sep (Algarve)

Sap burns skin in sunlight

Fig sap + sun = burns

The milky latex from leaves, stems and unripe fruit irritates skin. With sunlight it can cause blistering burns and dark patches that last months (phytophotodermatitis). Wear gloves and long sleeves. Wash any sap off with soap and water straight away, and keep that skin out of the sun for the rest of the day. People with latex allergy may react to figs.

Identify
A shrub or spreading tree with smooth grey bark and large, rough, thick leaves with 3–5 deep lobes. Any break oozes milky sap. The fruit is pear-shaped, green, purple or brown, with a small opening at the bottom.
Where
All over the Algarve: farms, walls, gullies and waste ground. In the NE US figs grow only as garden trees (fruit Aug–Oct).
Ripe
The fruit droops on a softened neck, feels soft, may split or show a drop of nectar at the bottom, and lifts off with little or no milky sap. Firm, upright fruit with milky sap at the stem is unripe, and figs barely ripen after picking. Some trees bear an early crop (brebas) in Jun–Jul; the main crop is Aug–Sep.
Harvest
Lift and twist gently, and lay the figs in one layer in a shallow tray. Wasps and ants share the tree, so look before you grab.
Keep
Fresh figs last 1–2 days, a little longer in the fridge. To dry them, halve them (or leave small figs whole) and lay them on screens in full sun under fine mesh against insects. Turn them daily and bring them in at night. After several days they should be leathery with no moist centre. Or dehydrate at about 135 °F (57 °C). They also freeze well. For jam, note figs are low in acid, so tested recipes add lemon juice.
Carob Ceratonia siliqua (alfarroba) · Aug–Oct, peak Aug–Sep (Algarve)

Discard the hard seeds

Identify
An evergreen tree with a dense, rounded crown. Its compound leaves have 2–5 pairs of round, leathery, glossy leaflets and no end leaflet. The pods are 4–12 in (10–30 cm) long, flat and leathery, green turning dark brown and glossy when ripe, with a sweet smell. Inside are very hard, flat brown seeds.
Where
Very common in the Algarve's dry limestone hills (barrocal) and farmland. It is a commercial crop, so ask the owner.
Harvest
Late summer, when the pods are dark brown and dry. The traditional way is to spread tarps or sheets under the tree and knock the pods down with a long pole. You can also pick up clean fallen pods. Skip mouldy ones.
Process
Chew a dry pod and spit out the seeds, which can crack teeth and jam grinders.
  • Powder: break the pods, remove every seed, and dry the pieces until brittle (in the sun or a low oven). Roast lightly for a darker flavour if you like, then grind and sieve.
  • Syrup: soak broken pods in water overnight, simmer, strain, and boil down.
Keep
Fully dry pods keep for months in a dry, insect-proof container. Moths and beetles love them; freeze for 48 hours if you see larvae.
Almond Prunus dulcis (amêndoa) · Jul–Sep, peak Aug (Algarve)

Bitter almonds are poisonous

Only sweet almonds are food

Bitter almonds contain amygdalin, which releases cyanide when chewed. A small handful can seriously poison a child. Many wild, feral and old seedling trees in the Algarve bear bitter almonds, and you can't tell by looking. If a raw kernel tastes bitter, spit it out, rinse your mouth, and don't eat from that tree. Roasting does not make bitter almonds safe. Never eat apricot, peach, plum or cherry kernels as "almonds" either.

Identify
A small tree with narrow, pointed, finely toothed leaves and pink or white blossom in Jan–Feb, before the leaves. The fruit is a flattened, velvety green oval, 1–2 in (3–5 cm). Its leathery hull dries and splits open when ripe, showing a pitted shell with one (sometimes two) kernels.
Test each tree
Adults only. Crack one nut, bite a tiny piece off the raw kernel, chew briefly and spit it out. Mild and sweet means a sweet almond. Bitter, burning, or a strong marzipan or cherry-pit flavour means bitter: leave that tree alone. Test every tree separately.
Harvest
Aug–Sep, when most hulls have split and are drying. Tap or shake the branches with a pole over a tarp. Don't thrash them, because that damages next year's fruiting spurs.
Hull, dry, crack
Strip off the hulls. Dry the nuts in the shell in the sun for several days to 2 weeks, until the kernel rattles and snaps cleanly. Crack with a nutcracker or a hammer on a stone; many Algarve almonds have very hard shells. Discard shrivelled, discoloured or mouldy kernels.
Keep
In the shell, kept dry and cool, up to a year. Store shelled nuts airtight, cool and dark, or in the fridge or freezer for long storage.
Olive Olea europaea (azeitona) · Sep–Jan, peak Oct–Nov (Algarve)

Inedible raw: must be cured Lye burns

Identify
An evergreen tree with a gnarled grey trunk. Narrow, leathery leaves grow in opposite pairs, grey-green above and silvery below. The fruit is an oval stone fruit, green ripening through purple to black. Wild olive (zambujeiro) has smaller leaves and small fruit with little flesh.
Raw
Raw olives are intensely bitter. They aren't dangerous, but they are inedible until cured.
Harvest
Green olives Sep–Oct; ripe purple-black olives Nov–Jan. Hand-pick, or comb the branches over a tarp. Discard bruised fruit and olives with tiny holes or brown spots (olive fly).
Containers
Glass, glazed ceramic, stainless steel or food-grade plastic. Never aluminium, copper or iron.

Simple water cure, then brine (green or turning olives)

  1. Wash the olives. Crack each one with a flat stone or mallet until the flesh splits to the pit (don't smash the pit), or cut 2–3 slits to the pit with a knife.
  2. Put them in your container and cover with clean water. Keep them under with a plate or a water-filled bag on top.
  3. Change the water every day; twice a day is faster. Cracked olives usually take about 1–2 weeks. Slit olives take longer.
  4. From about day 5, taste a small piece and spit it out. Stop when it is only mildly bitter, to your taste.
  5. Drain and cover with brine: 100 g salt per 1 litre of water (about 13 oz per US gallon). Add garlic, oregano, bay leaf or lemon slices if you like. They are ready to eat after about a week.
  6. Keep them in the fridge, fully covered by brine, and use within a few months. If they are too salty, soak them in fresh water for a few hours before serving. Throw out olives that turn mushy or slimy, smell off, or grow fuzzy mould.

Brine only: whole, uncut olives, especially ripe black ones, can go straight into the same brine. Change it every few weeks. This is slower, usually 3–6 months.

Lye (caustic soda) curing: tested recipe only

Commercial-style cures use food-grade sodium hydroxide. They take days rather than weeks, but lye can blind and burn in seconds. Don't improvise it off-grid, and never use home-made wood-ash lye of unknown strength. If you follow a tested recipe:

  • Wear goggles, rubber gloves and long sleeves. Keep children and pets away, and work outdoors or with good ventilation.
  • Add lye to cold water, never water to lye, which makes it boil and spatter. Use glass, stainless steel or food-grade plastic only: lye attacks aluminium and gives off flammable hydrogen.
  • If lye splashes you, flush skin with running water for at least 15–20 minutes, and eyes for at least 20 minutes, then get medical help.
  • Rinse the olives in many changes of fresh water over several days, as the recipe says, until no slippery feel remains.
Botulism

Olives are a low-acid food. Never can them in a water bath, and never store them in oil at room temperature. Keep cured olives in brine in the fridge. See the Food page.

Prickly pear Opuntia ficus-indica (figo-da-índia), O. humifusa (NE US) · fruit Jul–Oct · pads Mar–May

Glochids: tongs and gloves Hard seeds

Identify
A cactus with flat, oval, jointed pads. Pads and fruit carry large spines and tufts of tiny, barbed, hair-like spines (glochids) that come off at a touch and lodge in skin. Even "spineless" plants have glochids. The fruit is egg-shaped, 2–4 in (5–10 cm), growing on the pad edges and ripening to red, purple, orange or yellow-orange.
Where
Algarve: everywhere on dry ground, walls and field edges. NE US: the native eastern prickly pear is low and sprawling, with small red-purple fruit (Aug–Oct), on sandy coastal sites. Patches are small, so take very little.
Ripe
Full, deep colour, slightly soft, and twists off easily.
Gear
Kitchen tongs, thick leather gloves (or several layers) and a bucket. Stand upwind, because glochids blow about. Never rub your eyes.

Clean and peel the fruit

  1. Twist each fruit off with tongs and drop it in the bucket. Don't touch them bare-handed.
  2. Remove the glochids. Hold the fruit with tongs or a fork and singe it over a flame, turning until the hairs burn off. Or scrub it under running water with a stiff brush (gloves on). Or roll the fruit in sand or gravel with a broom, then rinse.
  3. On a board, hold the fruit with a fork. Cut off both ends, slit the skin lengthwise, and peel the thick skin back with a knife and fork.
  4. Eat the flesh, ideally chilled. The seeds are hard, so swallow them whole or press the pulp through a sieve for juice. Eating a lot of seeds at once can cause constipation or a blockage, so keep portions moderate.

Pads (nopales): in spring, cut young, bright green, palm-sized pads at the joint (tongs and gloves). Scrape off every spine cluster with a knife, trim the edge and rinse. Slice them, then boil until tender and rinse off the slime, or grill them.

Glochids in skin: don't rub. Pat sticky tape on and peel it off, or spread on white glue, let it dry and peel it. Use tweezers for big spines.

Toxic look-alikes

The rules that keep you alive
  • If it is not 100% identified, do not eat it.
  • Never eat white berries, or any berry you cannot name.
  • Aggregate berries (blackberry, raspberry, wineberry, dewberry) are among the safest groups for beginners, but still confirm the plant.
  • As a beginner, never forage anything in the carrot family (umbrella-shaped flower clusters, ferny leaves). Poison hemlock, water hemlock and hemlock water-dropwort kill, and a single bite can be enough.
  • Birds eating a berry proves nothing. Birds eat pokeweed, yew and holly berries.
  • Cooking does not make most poisonous plants safe.
  • Garden, hedge and park plants cause many poisonings, especially in children. Teach kids never to eat anything outdoors without asking an adult.
  • Check the plant the fruit is attached to. Vines and shrubs tangle together.

Harvesting technique & tools

Kit

  • Containers: shallow baskets or rigid tubs for soft fruit. A bucket hung from your belt or neck leaves both hands free. Use mesh or cloth bags for nuts so air can flow. Avoid closed plastic bags for anything warm or wet.
  • Tarp or old sheet, about 10 × 10 ft (3 × 3 m), for shaking.
  • Long pole (bamboo or similar) for tapping branches. A hooked stick (a forked branch or a cane with a hook) for gently pulling branches down.
  • Gloves: leather for chestnut burs, brambles and prickly pear; waterproof nitrile or rubber for walnut husks and fig sap.
  • Tongs (prickly pear), pruners or scissors (grape, elderberry and loquat clusters), and a small knife.
  • Hand rake or leaf rake to pull nuts out of leaf litter. A rolling nut gatherer (a wire cage on a handle) picks up walnuts, pecans, chestnuts and acorns without bending.
  • A hat and eye protection when shaking trees. Long sleeves and trousers, insect repellent and water.

Pole and tarp method

  1. Spread the tarp under the branch, out to the edge of the canopy. Clear away sticks and stones so nothing bruises.
  2. Put on a hat and eye protection, and stand clear of where things will fall. Shake the branch by hand, or tap it sharply with the pole near the fruit or nuts.
  3. Ripe nuts and fruit drop and unripe ones stay, which is the point. Don't thrash or break limbs, because that damages next year's crop.
  4. Gather up the corners and pour the harvest into containers. Pick out leaves, unripe fruit, and anything mouldy or holed.
  • Shaking works best for mulberries, serviceberries, almonds, carob, pecans and beechnuts.
  • Gathering from the ground is better for nuts and fruit that drop on their own as they ripen (acorns, walnuts, hickory, chestnuts, pawpaw, persimmon), because you get only ripe ones. Go daily or every other day: whatever lies around gets mouldy, wormy or eaten. Skip anything that has sat in wet leaves for days.
  • Pick by hand soft fruit that bruises (berries, figs, loquat, medronho), and hazelnuts before they fall.
Falls are the commonest foraging injury

Don't climb trees to forage. Use a stable stepladder only on level ground and with a helper. Never shake a branch you are standing under.

Ethics & the law

  • Take no more than about a third of what's on a plant or patch, and less if the patch is small. Spread your picking out.
  • Leave plenty for wildlife. Nuts and fruit are their winter food.
  • Don't damage plants. No breaking branches, stripping bark or pulling up roots.
  • Get permission on private land. In Portugal, olives, almonds, carob, figs, chestnuts and pine cones usually belong to a landowner, even on unfenced, untidy land.
  • Protected areas have rules on collecting: limits or outright bans. That includes many US state and national parks, and Portugal's natural parks (e.g. Ria Formosa, Costa Vicentina). Check before you pick, and never take rare or protected plants.

Where not to pick

Roadsides (exhaust, old lead, de-icing salt, herbicide), rail lines and power-line corridors (herbicide), sprayed fields and orchards, lawns, golf courses and parks, industrial sites, dumps and mine spoil, ditches and floodplains that take sewage or polluted runoff, and dog-walking edges. Wash everything in clean water.

Processing & storage

Drying

  • Sun drying works in hot, dry, sunny weather, such as an Algarve summer (figs, carob, almonds). Lay produce in a single layer on screens or racks off the ground, under fine mesh against insects and birds. Turn it daily and bring it in at night, because dew re-wets it. It takes days.
  • Solar dryer: a box with a dark base, a clear lid and screened vents top and bottom. Faster and cleaner than open sun.
  • Air drying in shade suits nuts in the shell. Use screens or mesh bags somewhere cool, dry, airy and safe from rodents.
  • Low oven, or near a stove: use the lowest setting with the door propped open, and stir often. Fruit and fruit leather dry well at 135–140 °F (57–60 °C). Dry nuts below about 100 °F (38 °C), because more heat speeds rancidity. Roasting is a separate, final step.
  • When is it done? Dried fruit is leathery with no moist centre when squeezed; nut kernels snap rather than bend. Then condition dried fruit: pack it loosely in a jar and shake daily for a week. Any condensation means it needs more drying.

Cold storage & freezing

  • Fresh fruit: keep in the fridge at 32–40 °F (0–4 °C), unwashed, in shallow containers. Most berries last 1–3 days; figs, mulberries and medronho 1–2.
  • Chestnuts: keep in the fridge in a breathable bag, and use within a few weeks.
  • Freezing: spread berries on a tray, freeze, then bag them so they stay loose. Freeze persimmon and pawpaw as seedless pulp, chestnuts cooked and peeled, and nut kernels in airtight bags (a year or more).

Simple preserving

  • Jam and jelly need enough sugar, acid and pectin to set and keep. Low-sugar jams must be refrigerated or frozen. Low-acid fruits such as figs, mulberries and elderberries need added lemon juice; follow tested recipes.
  • Canning: a water bath is safe only for high-acid foods (pH 4.6 or lower, such as most fruit and jams). Low-acid foods, including olives, need pressure canning. Use tested recipes and read the canning safety rules on the Food page. Throw out bulging, leaking or off-smelling jars without tasting.
  • Fruit leather: purée the fruit, sieve out the seeds, spread it ⅛–¼ in (3–6 mm) thick, and dry until it is no longer sticky.
  • Syrups (elderberry, rose hip, carob): keep in the fridge.

Nuts: rancidity, mould and insects

  • Rancidity: nut oils go rancid with heat, light and air. Rancid nuts smell of paint, crayons or old oil and taste sharp or bitter. Throw them out: they have lost their value and can upset the stomach.
  • Storage: keep nuts in the shell where possible, because the shell protects them. Stored cool, dry, dark and rodent-proof, they last months. Shelled nuts last only weeks at room temperature, months airtight in the fridge, and a year or more in the freezer.
  • Mould: throw out mouldy, shrivelled or discoloured kernels. Mould can produce aflatoxin, which cooking doesn't destroy.
  • Weevils and moth larvae: freeze nuts and dried fruit for at least 48 hours at 0 °F (−18 °C) to kill eggs and larvae, then store airtight. Larvae often crawl out of freshly gathered acorns and chestnuts, so process those quickly.

Universal edibility test: last resort only

Passing this test does not make a plant safe

It is a slow, risky last resort for a genuine survival emergency, and it only screens for fast, obvious reactions. Many plants that pass are still harmful. Some toxins are slow or cumulative: they damage the liver, kidneys or heart, and show up hours or days later, or only after repeated meals. Some plants (water hemlock, yew, oleander, castor bean) are so toxic that even the tiny test amounts can seriously harm or kill. Identification always beats testing.

Do NOT use it

  • Never on mushrooms or any other fungus.
  • If you have food, can wait for rescue, or can walk out. You can survive about 3 weeks without food; water and shelter matter far more.
  • If you can't spare 24+ hours, or you are already weak. The test costs energy and fluids.
  • On any plant with:
    • milky or discoloured sap;
    • a bitter or soapy taste (if one appears at any step, stop);
    • an almond scent in crushed leaves or woody parts;
    • umbrella-shaped flower clusters, or dill-, carrot- or parsley-like leaves (the deadly carrot family);
    • three-leaved growth (poison ivy and others);
    • beans, bulbs or seeds inside pods;
    • spines, fine hairs or thorns;
    • grain heads with pink, purple or black spurs (ergot);
    • wilted or mouldy parts.
  • On anything you have already identified as toxic, or that resembles a toxic plant on this page.
  • On children, or anyone pregnant or ill.

The procedure: one part of one plant at a time

  1. Pick one part to test: leaf, stem, root, bud, flower or fruit. A part that passes says nothing about the plant's other parts, or about the same part prepared another way. Test only plants that are plentiful, or it isn't worth the effort.
  2. Smell it. Reject anything with a strong, unpleasant or almond-like smell.
  3. Fast for 8 hours before you start. For the whole test, take nothing by mouth except purified water and the plant part you are testing.
  4. Skin contact test (do it during the fast): crush the part and hold it against the inside of your elbow or wrist. Watch for burning, itching, redness, rash or swelling for at least 15 minutes, and preferably the whole 8 hours. Any reaction: reject the plant.
  5. Prepare a small portion exactly as you plan to eat it, raw or boiled. Each preparation method needs its own test.
  6. Lip: touch a small piece to the outer surface of your lip and wait 3 minutes. Any burning, itching, numbness or tingling: stop and reject.
  7. Tongue: place a piece on your tongue and hold it there for 15 minutes. Don't chew or swallow.
  8. Chew and hold: chew a pinch thoroughly and hold it in your mouth for 15 minutes. Don't swallow. Bitter or soapy taste, burning, stinging or numbness: spit it out, rinse, and reject.
  9. Swallow that small amount, then wait 8 hours. Eat nothing else.
  10. No reaction at all? Eat a larger amount, about ¼ cup (60 mL), prepared the same way. Wait another 8 hours.
  11. Still no reaction: that one part, prepared that way, is probably safe in moderation. Start with small meals and watch for problems over the following days.

Total: about 24 hours or more for each plant part.

Stop at the first sign of a reaction

Nausea, cramps, dizziness, mouth irritation, a racing or pounding heart, or anything unusual: stop, spit out what is in your mouth, rinse, and eat no more. Do not make yourself vomit. Drink some water and follow the poisoning steps below.

Plant poisoning: signs and what to do

Early signs

Signs can start minutes or hours after eating; some much later.

  • Burning, tingling, numbness or swelling of the lips, mouth or throat; drooling, or a very dry mouth.
  • Nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, diarrhoea.
  • Dizziness, headache, sweating, weakness, trembling.
  • Big pupils, blurred vision, hot, dry, flushed skin, confusion, agitation or hallucinations (nightshades, datura).
  • A slow, fast or irregular heartbeat, or fainting (yew, oleander, lily-of-the-valley, foxglove).
  • Muscle twitching or seizures (water hemlock, hemlock water-dropwort).
  • Trouble breathing, hives, or swelling of the face or tongue: this is an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
  • On the skin: rash, blisters or burns (fig sap plus sun, glochids, irritant sap).

Some poisonings (castor bean, for example) start hours later, after a gap with no symptoms. Don't relax just because someone feels fine.

What to do

  1. Stop eating. Spit out what is in the mouth and rinse with water. Stop everyone else eating the same thing.
  2. Call emergency now on 911 (US) or 112 (Portugal/EU) if they have collapsed, are unresponsive, are having seizures, have trouble breathing or chest pain, or have a very slow or irregular pulse. If they have an epinephrine auto-injector and show signs of anaphylaxis, use it. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR (see First aid). If they are unresponsive but breathing, put them in the recovery position. With no signal, try anyway: emergency calls can connect through any network.
  3. Otherwise, call poison control right away, even if symptoms are mild: US Poison Control 1-800-222-1222 (24/7, free) · Portugal CIAV 800 250 250. Have ready: the person's age and weight, the plant (or a description), which part, how much, when, and the symptoms.
  4. Don't make them vomit unless poison control or a doctor tells you to. No salt water and no fingers down the throat. Give nothing by mouth to anyone drowsy, confused or seizing. Give activated charcoal only if advised to.
  5. Keep a sample of the plant (leaves, fruit, seeds) in a bag or paper, and take photos. Keep any vomit too. Bring it all to the hospital.
  6. Write down when they ate and when each symptom started. Watch them closely for at least 24 hours, and don't leave them alone.
  7. Skin or eyes: remove contaminated clothing and wash skin with soap and plenty of water. Keep fig-sap areas out of the sun. Rinse eyes with clean running water for 15 minutes.
