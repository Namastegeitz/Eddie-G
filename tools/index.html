SOS strobe & Morse light

Flashing light warning

The strobe flashes on and off. It can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy. Point it away from people near you.

Turn screen brightness all the way up first — a web page can't do it for you. To stop: tap anywhere on the light, or press Back / Esc.

How to signal with light

  1. Turn the screen brightness all the way up.
  2. Get to an open spot — a clearing, ridge, roof or shoreline — where searchers can see you.
  3. Point the screen (or flashlight) straight at the searchers: an aircraft, boat, vehicle or distant light. Hold the phone still.
  4. Signal when you see or hear searchers. Rest in between to save battery.
  5. In daylight a phone screen is hard to see. Use a signal mirror, bright clothing, or three of anything (3 fires, 3 rock piles).

Whistle & alarm tones

Protect hearing

Never hold the speaker to anyone's ear, including your own. These tones are made to be piercing.

Turn the phone's volume all the way up, then tap a sound. Tap it again (or Stop) to stop. One sound plays at a time.

Not playing.

Sound settings

Tone shape

Square sounds loudest on small phone speakers. Sine is the smoothest. Morse speed is set under Light settings.

Carry a real whistle

A pea-less plastic or metal whistle is louder than a phone speaker, works wet or frozen, and never runs out of battery. Keep one on your jacket zip.

Get the most from the phone

Volume all the way up. On iPhone, set the Ring/Silent switch to Ring — silent mode can mute web sounds. Point the speaker (usually the bottom edge) toward searchers. Human hearing is most sensitive at about 2–4 kHz, which is why whistles are pitched there.

Signalling conventions

  • Three of anything means distress: 3 whistle blasts, 3 flashes, 3 shots, 3 fires in a triangle. Pause, listen for a reply, repeat.
  • A common hiking and scouting whistle convention: 1 blast = “Where are you?” · 2 blasts = “Come to me” · 3 blasts = “Help / emergency”. It is a convention, not a law — agree it with your group beforehand.
  • UK and European mountains: the International Mountain Distress Signal is 6 blasts or flashes spread over one minute, a one-minute pause, then repeat. The reply is 3 per minute.
  • SOS (· · · — — — · · ·) is understood worldwide, by light or sound.

Contacts & radio

Radios: in the US, FRS radios (22 channels) need no licence; GMRS needs an FCC licence. In Europe, PMR446 radios (16 channels on 446 MHz) are licence-free. “Privacy codes” (CTCSS/DCS) only filter what you hear — anyone on that channel can still hear you. A text message often gets through when calls don't.

When thunder roars, go indoors

If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck. Get to shelter now — don't wait for rain or a close strike. Stay there until 30 minutes after the last thunder.

    Lightning safety (US National Weather Service guidance)

    • No place outside is safe when a thunderstorm is near.
    • Best shelter: an enclosed building with wiring and plumbing, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows up. Tents, picnic shelters and open sheds offer no protection.
    • Caught outside? These lower the risk a little; they don't make you safe — keep moving toward shelter:
      • Get off peaks, ridges and hilltops.
      • Stay away from isolated tall trees, cliffs and rock overhangs.
      • Get out of and away from water — lakes, rivers, ponds.
      • Stay away from metal fences, power lines and other things that conduct electricity.
      • Don't lie flat on the ground.
      • In a group, spread out so one strike can't injure everyone.
    • The old “lightning crouch” does not make you safe. Use the time to reach shelter.
    • Someone struck? They carry no electric charge — it is safe to touch them. Call for help. If they are not breathing normally, start CPR.
