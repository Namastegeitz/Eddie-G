Trapping, Fishing & Preserving

Getting protein in an emergency, cleaning it safely, and making food last. Water and shelter come first; food is a slower problem. For wild plant foods see Foraging; for drinking water see Water.

Hunting, trapping and fishing laws apply to you unless your life is genuinely at risk. Food-preservation mistakes can kill (botulism) — follow tested recipes exactly. When in doubt, throw it out.

Priorities & reality check

  • You can go weeks without food (rule of 3s, typical not absolute: 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather, 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food). Sort out water and shelter first.
  • Calories matter more than variety. Fat and carbohydrate (oil, nuts, grains, sugar) keep you warm and moving. Eat your stored food sensibly before gambling energy on wild food.
  • Count the cost. Hunting, trapping and fishing burn calories. A day's effort can easily yield less than it costs — especially for beginners.
  • Short on water? Eat sparingly, especially protein, which needs extra water to process.
  • Don't get sick from your food. Vomiting and diarrhoea cost water and strength you can't spare. Cook thoroughly; wash hands.
"Rabbit starvation" (protein poisoning)

Living on very lean meat alone — rabbit, squirrel, lean fish — without enough fat or carbohydrate causes nausea, diarrhoea, weakness and intense cravings, and you can starve while eating. Eat the fat, marrow and organs (liver, heart, kidneys) of healthy animals, and add fat or starch from any other source: oils, nuts, stored grains, starchy plants.

Trapping & snaring

Legal warning — read first

Trapping and snaring are illegal or tightly regulated in most places outside a genuine life-or-death emergency. Licences, seasons, permitted trap types, protected species and landowner permission all apply.

  • Snares are indiscriminate. They catch pets, deer, protected species — and can injure people.
  • Check every trap at least once a day. Leaving an animal suffering is cruel and, in most places, an offence.
  • Dispatch catches quickly and humanely (a fast, firm blow to the back of the head for small game).
  • Know where every set is. Remove them all when you leave.

Be honest about the odds: beginners usually catch little or nothing. Experienced trappers set many traps to get a few animals. Trapping only pays if you set several, at fresh sign, and check them daily — so it works best alongside fishing and plant foods, not instead of them.

Reading sign

  • Runs and trails: narrow, worn paths through grass and brush, under fences and fallen logs. Fresh = vegetation still bent, no cobwebs across it.
  • Droppings: fresh ones are moist and dark. Rabbits leave small round pellets in clusters.
  • Tracks in mud, sand and snow; feeding sign: gnawed bark, cleanly clipped twigs, opened nut shells.
  • Dens and burrows, and the routes between bedding, feeding and water. Animals use the same paths daily, often at dawn and dusk.
  • Funnels: places where the landscape narrows the route — gaps between rocks or bushes, under a leaning log, along a fence line, a narrow bank by water.

Channelling

Set traps where the animal has to pass. Use natural funnels first. Add a few sticks or bits of brush either side of the set to close gaps, so the easiest route is through the trap. Change as little as possible: disturbed ground, fresh-cut wood and human scent make animals detour. Work quickly, and handle sets with gloves or soil-rubbed hands.

Simple wire snare

  1. Find an active run with fresh sign, ideally at a natural narrow point.
  2. Make the noose: twist a small eye in one end of flexible snare wire (brass or stainless). Pass the other end through the eye to form a loop that slides freely.
  3. Size it: loop about a fist wide (≈ 4 in / 10 cm) for rabbit; bottom of the loop about a fist above the ground.
  4. Hang it across the run, facing along the trail, from a crossbar or anchor that is firmly fixed — a caught animal pulls hard.
  5. Close side gaps with guide sticks so the loop is the easiest way through.
  6. Check at least daily. Dispatch quickly. Remove or spring every snare when you are done.

Figure-4 deadfall

Three notched sticks hold up a heavy flat rock. An animal tugging the bait collapses the sticks and the rock drops. It works without wire, but takes practice to carve and tune.

  1. Cut three straight, dry, sound sticks. Thicker sticks for a heavier rock.
  2. Upright: carve the top to a flat chisel edge. Cut a square notch on one side, partway down, at trigger height.
  3. Diagonal: cut a notch near its top end so it sits on the upright's chisel top. Carve its lower end to a chisel edge.
  4. Trigger: cut a notch near one end to take the diagonal's chisel end, and a square notch where it crosses the upright so the two lock. Sharpen the other end for bait.
  5. Practise without the rock until it holds together and collapses at a light touch on the bait end.
  6. Set it by an active run. Lean a heavy flat rock (or log) so its raised edge rests on the diagonal's top and the bait end is under the rock. Close the sides with sticks so the animal must reach in from the front.
Fingers and hands

Deadfalls trip while you set them. Keep your hands, arms and head out from under the rock at all times — support it from the side and position the sticks from outside the fall zone. Practise with a light weight first. A heavy deadfall can break bones and can injure a child or pet that disturbs it: never set one near paths, camps or homes.

Fishing without a rod

Legal warning

Most places require a fishing licence and set seasons, size limits and catch limits. Set lines and trotlines, fish traps, spears and gigs, gorges, nets and night fishing are often restricted or banned, and live fish as bait is often banned (it spreads invasive species and disease). Check local rules. Never use poisons — plant or chemical — or explosives: illegal nearly everywhere, they kill everything downstream and can poison you.

Hand line on a bottle or stick

  1. Tie the line to the neck of a plastic bottle (or a can or stick) and wind it neatly around the body.
  2. Tie on a hook. Add a small weight a little above it (stone, nut, split shot) and a float if you want one (stick, cork, bit of foam).
  3. Cast: point the bottle's neck at your target and swing the weighted rig out — the line spirals off the end of the bottle like a spinning reel. With a stick, lay loose coils on clear ground and toss the rig.
  4. Retrieve by winding line back onto the bottle. Hold the line over a finger to feel bites; set the hook with a firm pull.

Line: fishing line from your kit, dental floss, strong thread, or the thin inner strands pulled from paracord.

Improvised hooks

  • Gorge: a sliver of bone or hard wood, about 1 in (2–3 cm) long for small fish (longer for bigger fish), sharpened at both ends, with a groove at the middle for the line. Bait it lengthwise. Let the fish swallow it before pulling. It usually kills the fish — no catch-and-release — and is banned in many places.
  • Thorn hook: cut a twig with a stout thorn (hawthorn, blackthorn) so the thorn points back up the shank; tie the line at the top.
  • Safety pin, paperclip or wire: bend into a J, sharpen the point on a stone. A pin's spring loop makes a ready eye.
  • Carved bone or hard wood hooks work but take time; keep them small.

Bait

  • Worms: dig in damp, rich soil; look under logs and stones after rain.
  • Insects: grasshoppers, crickets, beetles; grubs from rotting logs.
  • Fish scraps: eyes, guts and strips of belly from your first catch. Check its stomach to see what the fish are eating and copy that.

Where fish hold

  • Eddies and slack water behind rocks and logs; the seam where fast water meets slow.
  • Undercut banks, shade under overhanging trees, sunken wood and weed edges.
  • Drop-offs, deep pools, and where a riffle runs into a pool. Inflows and outlets of lakes.
  • Dawn and dusk are best. In hot weather fish go deeper and cooler; in cold weather they sit in slow, deep water.
  • Move quietly, keep your shadow off the water, and stay back from the edge.

Set lines (trotlines and bank poles)

A line with several baited hooks left in place — stretched between two anchors, or tied to a springy branch pushed into the bank. They fish while you do other work. Often regulated or banned — check the law. Check them at least daily (morning and evening is better), mark where they are, and remove every line and hook when you leave — abandoned lines kill birds, turtles and mammals.

Funnel fish traps

  1. Cut a large clear plastic bottle just below the shoulder.
  2. Remove the cap. Turn the top around and push it into the body so the neck points inward.
  3. Fasten with string, thorns or pins through both layers. Punch small holes so water flows through and it sinks.
  4. Bait it (bread, fish scraps, crushed snails), weight it with a stone, and set it in slow, shallow water near cover, tied to the bank.
  5. Check it every few hours. Fish find the funnel going in, but rarely find the narrow exit.
  1. In shallow water at the edge of a stream or lake, drive straight sticks or canes firmly into the bottom in a ring — gaps narrower than the fish you want.
  2. Leave one side open and build a funnel of stakes leading into it: wide at the mouth, with a narrow gap that ends inside the pen.
  3. Fill gaps at the bottom with stones; bait the pen if you like.
  4. Check it often. Close the gap with a stick and scoop fish out by hand or with a shirt used as a net.

Spearing and gigging

  • Split the end of a straight sapling into 2–4 prongs, wedge them apart, and sharpen them (barbs help). Lashing on a knife risks losing it.
  • Refraction: fish are deeper than they look. Aim low, below the fish you see. Approach slowly, keep still, strike from close.
  • Success rates are low, and wading in cold or fast water is dangerous. Spearing is banned for many species and waters.
Fish and shellfish that can poison you
  • Don't eat fish from polluted water — below mines, industry, sewage outfalls or heavy farmland, or where advisories are posted.
  • Wild mussels, clams and oysters: only from waters officially open for harvest. Algal toxins ("red tide") are not destroyed by cooking and can cause fatal paralysis.
  • Never eat pufferfish. Large tropical reef fish can carry ciguatera toxin, also not destroyed by cooking.

Cleaning fish & small game

Fish

  1. Kill quickly with a sharp blow to the top of the head behind the eyes. Cutting the gills to bleed it improves the meat.
  2. Keep it cool — shade, wet cloth, cold water — and clean it promptly.
  3. Scale if you'll eat the skin: scrape from tail to head with the back of a knife. Trout and small fish don't need it.
  4. Gut: put the knife point in the vent (small hole near the tail fin) and slit the belly forward to the gills, shallow so you don't cut the guts. Pull the guts out.
  5. Remove the gills — they spoil fastest.
  6. Scrape out the bloodline (dark red strip along the backbone) with a thumbnail or spoon. Rinse in clean water.

Fresh fish: clear, bulging eyes; red or pink gills; firm flesh that springs back; mild smell. Throw it out if eyes are sunken and cloudy, gills brown or grey, flesh soft, or it smells sour or of ammonia.

Cook thoroughly until the flesh is opaque and flakes — 145 °F (63 °C) inside. Freshwater fish carry parasites: never eat them raw.

Rabbit, hare and squirrel

Tularemia and sick animals

Wear gloves (nitrile or rubber) when cleaning rabbits and hares — tularemia (rabbit fever) infects through skin cuts, eyes and mouth. Don't touch your face. Don't eat any animal that acted sick — sluggish, easy to catch, staggering, mangy. Wash hands and tools well afterwards.

  1. Gloves on. Work on a clean surface away from your water source.
  2. Skin: pinch up the skin in the middle of the back and cut a small slit across it. Hook two fingers of each hand in and pull apart, toward the head and the tail. Cut off the head, feet and tail. (Squirrel skin is tougher; cut more and pull hard.)
  3. Gut: lay it on its back. Pinch up the belly wall and make a shallow cut. Guard the blade with two fingers inside and open from the pelvis to the ribs without puncturing the intestines or bladder — their contents taint the meat.
  4. Pull out the guts. Lift the bladder out intact. Cut around the vent to free the end of the gut.
  5. Check the liver: it should be smooth and evenly dark red-brown. White or yellow spots → discard the whole animal (bury or burn it) and scrub hands and tools. Discard also if the meat smells bad or has abscesses.
  6. Keep the heart, kidneys and a healthy liver — valuable fat and nutrients.
  7. Rinse with clean water and cool the meat fast: hang it in shade, or put it in a sealed bag in cold water. In warm weather cook or preserve it within hours.
  8. Bury guts and skins well away from camp and water.

Safe cooking temperatures (inside the thickest part)

FoodMinimum
Fish145 °F (63 °C) — opaque, flakes easily
Wild game, minced meat, bear and wild boar160 °F (71 °C) — no pink, juices clear
Poultry and wild birds165 °F (74 °C)

No thermometer: cook small game until no pink remains anywhere, including at the bone, and juices run clear. Bear, boar and other carnivore meat can carry trichinella that survives freezing — always cook it through.

Edible insects

High in protein and fat, and easier to catch than game. Grasshoppers, crickets, ants, termites, and beetle larvae (grubs from rotting wood) are common choices.

  1. Cook all insects — roast, boil or fry — to kill parasites and germs.
  2. Pull off the legs and wings of grasshoppers and crickets (spiny legs can stick in the throat).
  3. Start with a small amount: people allergic to shellfish or dust mites may react to insects.
  • Avoid brightly coloured, hairy or fuzzy, and foul-smelling insects, and caterpillars in general.
  • Never eat ticks, flies or mosquitoes (disease carriers), or insects from areas sprayed with pesticide.

Preserving basics

Germs and moulds need moisture, warmth, low acid and time. Every method takes one or more away: drying (water), salt (water), acid (fermenting, pickling), cold (cellar, cold water, snow), and heat plus a seal (canning). Smoke adds flavour and dries the surface — on its own it preserves very little.

The danger zone: 40–140 °F (4–60 °C)

Bacteria multiply fast in this range. Don't leave fresh meat, fish or cooked food in it for more than 2 hours (1 hour above 90 °F / 32 °C). Cook it, chill it, or start preserving it.

Drying & jerky

Jerky (USDA method)

  1. Choose lean meat and trim off every bit of visible fat — fat goes rancid.
  2. Partly freeze it for easier slicing. Slice thin, about 1/4 in (6 mm), against the grain.
  3. Marinate in the fridge or another cold place (salt, soy sauce, spices). Throw used marinade away.
  4. Heat the meat to 160 °F (71 °C) — poultry 165 °F (74 °C) — before drying (for example, simmer the strips in the marinade until they reach that temperature), or after drying if your tested recipe heats it then. This kills germs that low drying temperatures don't.
  5. Dry at 130–140 °F (54–60 °C) with good airflow — dehydrator, oven with the door propped open, or a carefully managed drying rack by the fire. Keep that temperature through the whole drying time.
  6. Done when a cooled piece bends and cracks, but doesn't snap.
  7. Blot off fat beads, cool, and pack in airtight containers or bags.

Storage: about 1–2 months at room temperature in an airtight container; longer in a fridge or freezer. Mould, slime or a rancid smell: discard.

Sun and air drying meat

Sun or air drying can't reliably reach safe temperatures and is slow in humid weather — meat can spoil before it dries. Heat the meat to 160 °F (71 °C) first. Wild game, especially bear and boar, must reach 160 °F.

Fruit and vegetables

  1. Use ripe, sound produce. Wash, peel if needed, and slice thin and even.
  2. Light-coloured fruit (apples, pears, peaches, bananas): dip slices briefly in diluted lemon juice (or a vitamin C solution) to slow browning.
  3. Most vegetables need brief blanching (steam or boil) before drying to stop enzyme changes — use the times in a tested drying guide.
  4. Dry at about 130–140 °F (54–60 °C) with airflow. A solar dehydrator (dark box, screen trays, vents, covered from insects) works in dry, sunny weather.
  5. Fruit is done when leathery and pliable with no moisture when squeezed; vegetables when brittle or crisp.
  6. Condition fruit: pack loosely in a closed jar for about a week, shaking daily. Any moisture on the glass → dry it more.
  7. Store airtight, cool, dark and dry.

Smoking

Hot smoking — use this

Smokes and cooks at once (USDA: smoker around 225–300 °F / 107–149 °C). Cook to an internal 145 °F (63 °C) for fish and 160 °F (71 °C) for game.

Cold smoking — not for beginners

Smoke below cooking temperature flavours food but does not cook it. Without correct curing salts and a tested recipe it is a real botulism and listeria risk, especially for fish.

Hot-smoked food is cooked food, not shelf-stable

Eat it within a few days, keep it cold, or freeze it — or dry it fully as jerky. Never seal home-smoked fish airtight and keep it at room temperature (botulism).

Simple tripod smoker

  1. Clear the ground of anything flammable. Never smoke food inside a tent or closed shelter — carbon monoxide kills.
  2. Lash three poles into a tripod roughly head height. Build a rack of green, non-toxic sticks across it at about waist height above a small fire pit.
  3. Salt or brine the meat or fish per a tested recipe, then lay thin strips or split fish on the rack.
  4. Wrap the tripod in a tarp, canvas, bark or brush, leaving a small gap at the top for draught.
  5. Keep a small, low, steady fire — coals topped with green or damp hardwood chips for smoke. Watch for flare-ups.
  6. Check internal temperature in the thickest piece (145 °F / 63 °C fish, 160 °F / 71 °C game). No thermometer: cook until fully opaque and firm throughout, then treat it as cooked food.

A long trench or pipe between the fire and the chamber cools the smoke — that is a cold-smoking design. Skip it unless you already know safe cold smoking.

What to burn

  • Good: hardwoods — oak, hickory, maple, beech, alder, apple, cherry and other fruit woods.
  • No resinous softwoods (pine, spruce, fir) — sooty, bitter.
  • No treated, painted, varnished or glued wood, plywood, or pallets of unknown origin.
  • No poisonous plants: oleander, yew, poison ivy/oak/sumac, laurels — or any wood you can't identify.

Salting & curing

Dry-salting fish

  1. Very fresh fish only. Gut it, remove the head, split it flat, scrape out the bloodline, rinse and drain. Always gut first.
  2. Use plenty of pure salt — coarse, pickling or sea salt without additives.
  3. In a non-metal container (food-grade plastic, wood, glazed crock): a layer of salt, a layer of fish skin-side down, salt over every surface, and repeat. Finish with salt.
  4. Put a weight on top and keep it as cool as you can. The salt draws out water as brine.
  5. Leave for days to weeks depending on thickness and temperature, adding salt if bare patches appear, until the flesh is firm and translucent all the way through.
  6. Brush off the salt and dry the fish in a cool, breezy, covered place, protected from rain, dew and flies, until stiff.
  7. Store wrapped, cool and dry.
Never salt-cure ungutted fish

Whole, ungutted salted or dried fish have caused fatal botulism outbreaks. Gut it first, keep it cold while the salt works, and discard anything slimy, pink-tinged, mouldy or off-smelling.

Brining

Soaking in salty water adds flavour and helps before smoking or drying. Brine alone does not make meat shelf-stable — keep brining meat cold and follow a tested recipe.

Curing salt (nitrite) — don't improvise

Nitrite is toxic in excess

Proper meat curing (ham, bacon, sausages, anything cold-smoked) uses nitrite curing salt ("Cure #1", "Prague powder") in exact amounts from a tested recipe, weighed on a scale. Too little risks botulism; too much is poisonous. Never guess the dose. Curing salt is often dyed pink — don't confuse it with table salt or pink Himalayan salt, and keep it away from children.

Desalting before eating

Soak salted fish or meat in cold water, kept cold, changing the water several times — about a day or two for thick salt fish. Taste a small cooked piece. Very salty food needs extra drinking water: don't eat it when water is short.

Root cellar & cold storage

Most root crops keep best at roughly 32–40 °F (0–4 °C) and 85–95% humidity, dark, with some ventilation. Don't let them freeze.

CropConditionsNotes
PotatoesCold, humid, darkCure first (below). Light turns them green — cut away green parts and sprouts, which are toxic.
Carrots, beets, turnips, parsnipsCold, humidTwist off tops leaving a short stub. Pack in damp sand, sawdust or leaves.
CabbageCold, humidStrong smell — keep it away from apples, or store it outside the cellar in a clamp.
Apples, pearsCold, humidKeep away from potatoes, carrots and cabbage: their ethylene gas makes potatoes sprout and carrots bitter.
Onions, garlicCool and dryNot in a damp cellar. Cure (dry) skins well first; hang in mesh.
Winter squash, pumpkins, sweet potatoesWarmer, about 50–60 °F (10–15 °C), and drierToo cold damages them. A cool room or closet, not the cellar.
  1. Store only mature, undamaged produce. Don't wash — brush off soil. Use bruised or cut pieces first.
  2. Cure potatoes in the dark at moderate temperature for about 2 weeks so skins toughen and nicks heal.
  3. Ventilate: cold air in low, warm air out high. In autumn, open vents on cold nights and close them on warm days.
  4. Check every week. Remove anything soft, sprouting, mouldy or rotting — rot spreads. Watch for rodents.

No cellar? Bury it

  • Buried container: sink a clean barrel or bin (food-grade if produce touches it) on its side or upright in well-drained ground, rim just above the surface. Layer produce with straw or dry leaves, lid on, then heap soil and straw over the top. Dig a drain trench around it.
  • Clamp (pit): see diagram.

In mild-winter climates (such as southern Portugal) the ground and air rarely get cold enough. Expect much shorter storage, and eat the most perishable crops first.

Canning & botulism

The rule that matters

Water-bath canning is ONLY for high-acid foods (pH 4.6 or lower): most fruits, jams, pickles, and tomatoes with added acid. Low-acid foods — vegetables, meat, fish, beans, soups — must be pressure canned. Use tested recipes (USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning / National Center for Home Food Preservation). This page deliberately gives no processing times: they depend on the food, jar size, packing method and altitude, and must come from a tested recipe.

Botulism spores survive boiling. In high-acid food they can't grow; in low-acid food sealed without oxygen they can, making a deadly toxin with no smell or taste. That is why acidity and method must match a tested recipe — don't add extra onions, peppers or other low-acid ingredients to one.

Water-bath canning basics

  1. Pick a tested recipe. Note its processing time, jar size, headspace and altitude adjustments before you start.
  2. Jars: proper canning jars with no chips or cracks. New flat lids every time (bands can be reused). Wash in hot soapy water and keep jars hot. If the recipe's processing time is under 10 minutes, sterilise the empty jars first (boil 10 minutes, plus 1 minute for each 1,000 ft / 300 m above 1,000 ft).
  3. Prepare the food exactly as written. Acidify tomatoes with bottled lemon juice or citric acid as directed (bottled juice has consistent acidity).
  4. Fill hot jars, leaving the headspace in the recipe (commonly 1/4 in / 6 mm for jams and jellies, 1/2 in / 12 mm for fruit, tomatoes and pickles). Remove air bubbles with a non-metal utensil.
  5. Wipe rims clean, put on lids, screw bands fingertip-tight.
  6. Jars on a rack in the canner, covered by at least 1 in (2.5 cm) of water. Lid on the canner.
  7. Start timing at a full rolling boil and keep it boiling. If the boil stops, bring it back and restart the time from zero.
  8. Altitude: above 1,000 ft (300 m), increase the time using the recipe's altitude table.
  9. When time is up, turn off the heat, remove the canner lid, wait 5 minutes, then lift jars straight up onto a towel, spaced apart, out of draughts.
  10. Leave undisturbed for 12–24 hours. Don't tighten bands or tip jars.
  11. Check seals: the lid is curved down and doesn't flex when pressed. Unsealed jars: refrigerate and eat soon, or reprocess within 24 hours with a new lid.
  12. Remove bands, wash jars, label with contents and date. Store cool (50–70 °F / 10–21 °C), dark and dry. Use within about 12–18 months.

Off-grid, canning needs a lot of fuel and water and a steady heat source; plan for it, or prefer drying and cold storage.

Pressure canning (low-acid foods)

Low-acid foods need temperatures only a pressure canner reaches. Use a tested recipe for time and pressure, adjust for altitude, and have a dial gauge tested every year. Electric pressure cookers and multicookers are not recommended for canning.

Unsafe methods — never use
  • Water-bath or "boiling" for low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, fish, beans, soups)
  • Open-kettle canning — filling hot jars and sealing without processing
  • Oven canning, microwave or dishwasher "canning"
  • Inversion — turning jars upside down to seal them
  • Untested, made-up or very old recipes and times
Botulism — warning signs and symptoms

Jar warning signs: bulging or swollen lid, leaking, unsealed, liquid spurting when opened, foaming, mould, cloudy liquid in a normally clear product, or an off smell. Many contaminated jars look and smell normal.

Symptoms, usually 12–36 hours after eating (can be a few hours to about 10 days): blurred or double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, dry mouth, muscle weakness, difficulty breathing, and paralysis that spreads downward from the head. This is a medical emergency — call 911/112 immediately. Antitoxin works best early.

  • Never taste food to test it. A tiny amount can kill.
  • Discard bulging, leaking or spurting jars without opening or tasting. Wear gloves, seal them in a heavy bag, and dispose of them where people and animals can't reach them. Wash hands and any surfaces they touched.
  • When in doubt, throw it out.

Fermenting & pickling

Lacto-fermented vegetables (sauerkraut, sour pickles)

  1. Use fresh, clean vegetables and a glass, food-grade plastic or glazed crock container — not metal, and not old crocks that may have lead glaze.
  2. Salt at about 2–3% by weight: a 2–3% brine is 20–30 g salt per litre of water. For shredded cabbage, mix the salt in and pound until it releases its own juice.
  3. Keep everything under the brine — weight it with a plate and a clean stone or a water-filled bag. Exposed vegetables go mouldy.
  4. Ferment at cool room temperature, covered to keep insects out, for days to weeks until pleasantly sour.
  5. Then store cold (fridge, cellar, cold water), still under brine.

Normal: bubbles, cloudy brine, sour smell, a thin white film on the surface (skim it off). Discard if there is fuzzy, pink or black mould through the food, the vegetables are soft or slimy, or it smells rotten.

Vinegar pickles

  • Use vinegar labelled at least 5% acidity. Never dilute it more than the recipe says — the acid is the safety margin. Homemade vinegar has unknown strength: don't use it for canned pickles.
  • Use canning/pickling salt and a tested recipe.
  • For shelf storage, process in a water bath per the recipe. Unprocessed "refrigerator pickles" must stay cold.
