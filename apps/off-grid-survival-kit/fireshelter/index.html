Fire, Shelter & Knots

Get out of wind and wet first. Get off the cold ground. Then make fire. Every method below is numbered so you can follow it with cold hands.

Read this first: practise these skills before you need them. Fire can kill you through burns, wildfire or carbon monoxide. Obey local fire bans. Knots here are for camp use — anything that holds a person's weight (climbing, rescue, lowering) needs proper training and rated gear.

Priorities

Rule of 3s (typical, not absolute): you can last about

  • 3 minutes without air (or in icy water, or with severe bleeding)
  • 3 hours without shelter in harsh weather
  • 3 days without water
  • 3 weeks without food

So in cold, wet or windy weather, shelter usually comes before fire. A fire can be rained out, burn down while you sleep, or run out of fuel. A good shelter keeps working all night.

  1. Stay dry. Wet clothing loses most of its insulation and pulls heat from you fast. Avoid sweating: slow down and open layers before you get hot.
  2. Get out of the wind. Use terrain, thick trees, boulders, or your pack and gear as a windbreak.
  3. Insulate from the ground. This matters more than a roof over your head. Cold ground steals heat far faster than cold air. Pile up a thick, springy bed of dry leaves, pine needles, grass or boughs — at least a hand-span thick once compressed.
  4. Then overhead cover, then fire. Fire is for warmth, drying out, boiling water, signalling and morale.
Tip

Collect everything — insulation, fuel, water — before dark. Night work in the cold is slow, clumsy and dangerous.

Fire safety & choosing a site

Danger — wet rocks can explode

Never build a fire ring from wet, porous or river-bed rocks. Water trapped inside turns to steam and the rock can crack violently and throw hot fragments. Use dry rocks from high ground, or no ring at all. Do not sleep right against a rock face you have heated: it can flake off (spall).

  1. Check the rules. Obey fire bans and restrictions. In a true emergency, keep the fire as small as will do the job.
  2. Clear a circle of about 10 ft (3 m) down to bare mineral soil: no leaves, needles, dry grass or roots that can carry fire.
  3. Look up. No overhanging branches, dry foliage or tarps above the fire.
  4. Avoid peat, deep duff and root mats. Fire can creep underground and pop up later, metres away.
  5. Mind the wind. Sparks travel downwind. Put the fire downwind of your shelter and gear, and do not light it in strong wind.
  6. Keep water, a pot of soil or a shovel ready before you light anything.
  7. Never leave a fire unattended — not even for a few minutes.

Put it out completely

  1. Drown it with water (or damp soil if water is scarce — not dry leaves).
  2. Stir the ashes and embers, scrape partly burnt sticks, and drown again.
  3. Feel with the back of your hand just above, then on, the ashes. If anything is warm, repeat. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.

Gather twice what you think

Before you strike anything, gather at least twice the tinder and kindling you think you need, sorted by size within arm's reach. Fires usually die in the first two minutes because the next size wasn't ready.

Dry fuel in wet weather

  • Standing dead wood and dead branches still on trees — drier than anything on the ground. If it bends instead of snapping, it's too wet.
  • Split it. The core of a wet stick is often dry. Split wrist-thick dead wood with a knife and a baton.
  • Feather sticks: shave thin curls down a dry split stick, leaving them attached. They catch like tinder.
  • Birch bark (papery outer bark) burns even when damp — peel it from dead or fallen trees where you can.
  • Fatwood: resin-soaked wood from dead pine stumps and branch joints. Smells of turpentine; shavings light even when wet.
  • Dry grass, cattail fluff, old man's beard lichen — dry inside your clothing if damp.
  • From your kit: cotton balls smeared with petroleum jelly (pull apart before lighting), char cloth, wax, hand sanitiser (alcohol gel — burns with a near-invisible flame).
  • Build on a platform of dry sticks laid side by side, not on wet ground or snow.
Make char cloth catches a spark from flint & steel or a ferro rod
  1. Cut 100% cotton (old jeans, T-shirt, flannel) into squares about 2 in (5 cm). Synthetics melt — they won't work.
  2. Put them in a small metal tin with a lid (mint or shoe-polish tin). Punch one small hole in the lid with a nail.
  3. Put the tin in a fire or on coals. Smoke and gas will jet from the hole — sometimes alight. That's normal.
  4. When the smoke stops (often 5–10 minutes), remove the tin and let it cool completely before opening. Plug the hole with a twig while it cools so air can't get in.
  5. Good char cloth is black all through and flexible, not brown (undercooked) or crumbling to ash (overcooked). Store it dry in the tin.

Fire without matches

Carry three ways to light a fire (e.g. lighter, ferro rod, waterproof matches) and dry tinder in a sealed bag. The methods below are for when those fail. All of them make an ember or spark that you grow in a tinder bundle:

  1. Make a fist-sized "bird's nest" of fine, dry tinder: coarser material outside, finest fibres in the middle.
  2. Put the ember or burning material in the centre and fold the nest loosely around it.
  3. Hold it up, below and upwind of your face, and blow gently and steadily. Smoke thickens, then it flames.
  4. Place it at once under your kindling (already laid — see fire lays).
Ferro rod (ferrocerium) most reliable; works wet, in wind and at altitude
  1. New rods have a black coating — scrape it off first until you get sparks.
  2. Prepare a tinder bundle, cotton-and-petroleum-jelly ball, fatwood shavings or birch bark shavings on a dry base.
  3. Put the rod tip right in or against the tinder.
  4. Hold the striker (or the 90° spine of a knife — never the cutting edge) still, pressing firmly on the rod at a steep angle.
  5. Pull the rod back toward you while the striker stays put. This keeps your hand from smashing the tinder and puts sparks exactly where you aimed.
  6. Use slow, firm, full-length scrapes. Once the tinder catches, feed kindling.
Bow drill friction fire — hard work; practise in good conditions first
  1. Wood: dead, bone-dry, fairly soft wood — cedar, willow, poplar/aspen/cottonwood, basswood (linden), yucca stalk. Using the same wood for spindle and fireboard usually works. Your thumbnail should just dent it.
  2. Spindle: straight, about thumb-thick and a hand-span long (roughly 2 cm × 20 cm). Round the bottom; point the top slightly.
  3. Fireboard: flat, about finger-thick (1.5–2 cm), wider than the spindle.
  4. Bow: a stiff stick about arm-length with cord (shoelace, paracord) tied so the spindle twists into it tightly.
  5. Bearing block: hardwood, stone with a dimple, or bone. Lubricate its socket (grease, soap, lip balm, crushed green leaves) so friction happens at the bottom, not the top.
  6. Kneel, foot on the board, wrist braced against your shin so the spindle stays vertical. Burn a shallow depression near the board's edge.
  7. Cut the notch: a V from the board's edge to the centre of the depression, about 1/8 of the circle. Put a dry leaf or bark under it.
  8. Drill with long, full strokes using the whole bow. Start steady, then add speed and downward pressure as smoke builds and dark powder fills the notch.
  9. When there is heavy smoke and the powder keeps smoking on its own, stop. Let it sit a few seconds; fan gently. Tap the board and lift it away.
  10. Move the ember on its leaf/bark into the tinder bundle and blow gently until it flames.
Caution

This takes a lot of energy and sweat. In the cold, sweat chills you — work in short bursts and open layers. Don't burn energy on it if you have any other option.

Lens (sunlight) only in strong, direct sun
  1. Lens options: magnifying glass; reading glasses with strong magnifying (positive) lenses — glasses for short-sightedness will not work; a clear water-filled bottle or clear plastic bag twisted into a ball; or a lens shaped from very clear ice, smoothed with warm hands (cloudy ice will not work).
  2. Use dark tinder — char cloth, punky wood, or tinder blackened with charcoal catches far faster.
  3. Angle the lens square to the sun. Move it toward and away from the tinder until the spot is the smallest, brightest point.
  4. Hold it perfectly still and shield it from wind. When it glows, transfer to a tinder bundle and blow gently.

Don't stare at the bright spot for long. Midday sun works best; hazy or low sun often fails.

Battery + steel wool fast and easy if you carry both
  1. Use fine steel wool (grade 0000). Fluff out a pad on top of your tinder bundle.
  2. 9 V battery: touch both terminals to the wool at the same time. It glows and spreads instantly.
  3. AA / D cells: hold two or more end to end (+ to −), and touch a thin strip of wool across the two free ends.
  4. Fold the tinder around the glowing wool and blow gently.
Fire risk in your pack

Store steel wool sealed, away from batteries (separate bag or tin). A 9 V battery touching loose steel wool in a pack can start a fire.

Other methods flint & steel, fire piston, chemical
  • Flint & steel: strike a high-carbon steel striker down along the sharp edge of a hard rock (flint, chert, quartz) with char cloth held on top of the rock. Sparks land on the cloth and make a glowing spot; put it in a tinder bundle and blow.
  • Fire piston: a cylinder and plunger. Put char cloth in the plunger tip, drive it in hard and fast, then pull out at once — compressed air heats enough to make an ember.
  • Chemical: some kits use potassium permanganate with glycerin, which ignites by itself after a delay. It is a strong oxidiser and can flare violently. Use only if you have practised with it and follow the kit's instructions.

Fire lays

Lay the kindling before you make the spark or ember. Leave gaps for air. Light from the upwind side so the wind pushes flame into the fuel.

Teepee fast start, hot flame — best for lighting and boiling
  1. Put the tinder bundle on a dry base.
  2. Lean pencil-lead and pencil-thick sticks around it in a cone; leave a door facing the wind.
  3. Add a looser outer layer of thumb-thick sticks.
  4. Light through the door. Add fuel to the outside as it burns; it collapses into coals.
Log cabin stable, lots of coals — cooking and long burns
  1. Build a small teepee of tinder and kindling.
  2. Lay two thumb-to-wrist-thick sticks on either side, then two across them, like a cabin. Keep going, using slightly smaller sticks.
  3. Optionally lay a "roof" of kindling across the top.
  4. Light the teepee inside.
Lean-to fire lay for wind — a log shelters the tinder
  1. Lay a large log (or rock that is dry) on the upwind side.
  2. Put the tinder against its downwind side.
  3. Lean kindling from the ground onto the log, over the tinder.
  4. Light from the end, keeping your body as an extra windbreak.
Star fire saves fuel; long, slow burn with no chopping
  1. Start a small fire.
  2. Lay 4–6 long logs with their ends meeting in the fire, like spokes.
  3. Push the logs in as the ends burn. Pull them out to cut the heat.
Dakota fire hole low visibility, strong draught, good in wind
  1. Dig a fire hole roughly 1 ft (30 cm) wide and deep.
  2. About a hand-span or two away, on the upwind side, dig a smaller air hole angled down to meet the bottom of the fire hole.
  3. Build the fire in the bottom of the fire hole. Air is drawn through the tunnel.
  4. Pots can sit across the top on green sticks.
Don't dig in peat or roots

Never dig a Dakota hole in peat, deep duff or a mat of roots: fire can smoulder underground and spread unseen. Use mineral soil only. Refill the holes and soak them when done.

Long fire warms your whole body when sleeping out
  1. Lay two long logs side by side about a hand-span apart, as long as your body.
  2. Build the fire in the gap along its full length. Feed it to keep an even bed of coals.
  3. Sleep parallel to it with a clear, swept gap (about 3 ft / 1 m or more) — never downwind, and with nothing burnable between you and the fire.
  4. Someone stays awake to tend it, or you wake regularly. Pair it with a reflector wall behind the fire.
Reflector wall throws heat back toward you and your shelter
  1. Drive two pairs of stakes about 1–2 ft (30–60 cm) behind where the fire will be.
  2. Stack green or damp logs between them to knee-to-waist height. A dry rock face also works, but heat it gently and don't sleep against it.
  3. Build the fire between the wall and your shelter. Heat that would escape is bounced toward you.

Using fire

Signalling

  • Three fires in a triangle (or a straight line), spaced well apart (roughly 75–100 ft / 25–30 m), is an internationally recognised distress signal. Three of anything = help.
  • Daytime: smoke is seen better than flame. Pile green vegetation (fresh leaves, boughs, moss) on a hot fire for thick white smoke — best against dark forest.
  • Over snow or pale ground, black smoke shows better: burn a little rubber or oil (spare tyre strips, oily rags). Stay upwind — that smoke is toxic.
  • Night: bright flame. Keep signal fires built and ready (dry tinder protected under bark, green boughs nearby) so you can light them the moment you hear an aircraft.
  • Use open ground or a clearing so smoke rises and the fire can't spread.

Cooking & water

  • Cook over coals, not flame — steadier heat, less burning. A log cabin lay makes good coals.
  • Boil water at a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m). See Water.
  • Never put sealed cans or containers in a fire — they can burst.

Drying clothes safely

  • Hang wet items on sticks beside the fire, not over it, and never leave them unattended. Turn them often.
  • Synthetics melt and fleece can catch fire quickly; feel them often. Keep boots back — leather cracks and soles delaminate if too hot.
  • Dry socks and gloves first, then base layers. Drying clothes on your body near a fire also works but takes time.
  • Never bring a fire, stove or coals inside a tent or snow shelter to dry things. See carbon monoxide.

Choosing a shelter site

Look up, look down

Widowmakers — dead trees and dead or hung-up branches — kill campers every year, often in wind at night. Never shelter under or within falling distance of them. Never shelter in a dry wash, gully or stream bed: flash floods can arrive from rain you can't see or hear.

  • Avoid valley bottoms and hollows — cold air sinks and pools there at night, and they collect fog, frost and water.
  • Avoid exposed ridgetops — wind and lightning. A flat bench partway up a slope is often warmest.
  • Near materials and water, but not right beside water — a short walk away (roughly 200 ft / 60 m): drier, fewer insects, safer from rising water and animals coming to drink.
  • Use natural windbreaks: boulders, thickets, the lee side of a ridge or a big fallen log (check it's stable).
  • Drainage: a slight rise with firm, dry ground. Water must run away from your bed, not into it.
  • Avoid avalanche and rockfall paths, ant nests, animal trails and lone tall trees.
  • If you want to be found, stay near an open area where you can lay out signals and build signal fires.

Debris hut & lean-to

Debris hut no tools or tarp needed; warm without a fire
  1. Ridgepole: a sturdy pole about 1.5× your height. Rest one end on a stump, rock or forked prop about hip-to-waist high; the other end on the ground.
  2. Check the size: lie under it. You want just enough room to roll over — small is warm; your body heats the space.
  3. Ribs: lean sticks closely along both sides of the ridgepole, steep enough to shed rain. Leave a small entrance at the high end.
  4. Lattice: lay brushy twigs across the ribs so the debris doesn't fall through.
  5. Debris: pile dry leaves, needles, grass and ferns on top — at least 2–3 ft (60–90 cm) thick, more in cold or rain. It takes far more than you think. Top with branches to stop it blowing away.
  6. Bed: fill the inside with a thick layer of dry debris and compress it. You'll lie on and under debris — insulation underneath matters most.
  7. Door: keep the entrance small and facing away from the wind (leeward). Once inside, plug it with a pile of debris or a stuffed pack.
No fire inside

Never have a fire, stove or candle in or near a debris hut — it is a pile of dry fuel.

Lean-to quick; best with a fire and reflector in front
  1. Check the wind. Plan for the back of the roof to face into it. (Wind can shift at night — in valleys cold air often flows downhill.)
  2. Lash or wedge a ridgepole between two trees or on two forked uprights, about waist-to-chest high.
  3. Lean poles from the ridgepole to the ground on the windward side, fairly steep so rain runs off.
  4. Cover with boughs, bark or debris, starting at the bottom and overlapping upward like roof tiles. Make it thick.
  5. Build a thick bed of dry debris under the roof.
  6. Build a fire in front of the open side with a reflector wall beyond it. Keep a clear gap between bed and fire.

Snow shelters

Snow is a good insulator: inside a well-built snow shelter it often stays near freezing even when it is far colder outside. They take hours and make you wet — work slowly to avoid sweating, and wear waterproof outer layers.

Danger — suffocation & carbon monoxide

Always keep a ventilation hole open and check it often, especially while snow is falling or drifting. Inner walls glaze over and seal as you breathe and warm the space.

Never run a stove, heater, grill or generator inside a tent, snow shelter, vehicle or home: carbon monoxide kills. Even a candle burns oxygen and gives off some CO — only with the vent open, and never left lit while you sleep.

Keep a digging tool inside with you in case of collapse or drifting at the entrance. Build away from avalanche slopes and their run-out zones.

Quinzhee (snow mound) works with any snow, even shallow or powdery
  1. Pile snow into a mound about 6–7 ft (2 m) high and 8–10 ft (2.5–3 m) across. Mix and stir the snow as you pile it — it helps it set.
  2. Let it sinter (settle and harden) for 1–2 hours, longer in very cold, dry snow. Rest, eat and drink meanwhile — don't get sweaty.
  3. Push dozens of sticks about 1 ft (30 cm) long into the mound all over, pointing at the centre. These are depth gauges.
  4. Dig a low entrance on the downwind side. Tunnel in, then hollow out upward and outward.
  5. Stop digging wherever you reach the end of a stick: that leaves walls about 30 cm (1 ft) thick.
  6. Leave a raised sleeping platform above the entrance level. Cold air sinks into the low entrance trench, away from you.
  7. Smooth the ceiling into a dome so drips run down the walls instead of onto you.
  8. Push a fist-sized vent hole through the roof at an angle. Keep a stick in it and wiggle it clear regularly.
  9. Insulate the platform (pad, boughs, pack). Partly block the door with a pack or snow block — never airtight. Mark the outside with a pole or bright item.
Snow trench fastest snow shelter in firm, deep snow
  1. Mark a trench a little longer than you and about shoulder-width.
  2. Dig down about 3 ft (1 m) in firm snow, keeping walls straight. Cut blocks if the snow allows.
  3. Roof it: lean snow blocks together in an A shape, or lay branches, skis or a tarp across and cover with snow.
  4. Make the entrance at one end, lower than your bed, so cold air drains out of the sleeping area.
  5. Insulate the floor, open a vent, keep your digging tool inside.
Tree-well shelter under big conifers where snow is shallow by the trunk
  1. Choose a large evergreen with low, dense branches. Check for dead branches and heavy snow loads above.
  2. Dig down beside the trunk into the well under the branches.
  3. Line the floor thickly with boughs; add boughs over gaps in the roof.
  4. Keep any fire outside and away from the branches — heat melts the snow above, which can dump onto you or the fire.
Tree wells can trap you

Deep, loose snow around trees can collapse inward. People who fall in head-first can suffocate. Approach carefully, and never dig a deep well alone in loose powder.

Tarp set-ups

Run a taut ridgeline between two trees (use a bowline at one end and a taut-line hitch or trucker's hitch at the other). Point the lowest, most closed side into the wind. Pitch the tarp tight so rain runs off.

Staying warm

  1. Insulate underneath first. Pad, thick boughs, leaves, your pack under your legs. You lose heat into the ground far faster than into the air.
  2. Layers: a wicking base (wool or synthetic — cotton stays wet and cold), an insulating middle, a wind- and waterproof shell. Add and remove layers to stay just cool, not sweaty.
  3. Vent before you sweat. Open zips and remove a layer before hard work. Sweat freezes and soaks insulation.
  4. Cover head, neck, hands and feet. Change into dry socks and base layer for sleeping; keep one dry set just for that.
  5. Eat and drink before sleeping. A snack with fat and carbs fuels heat through the night. Dehydration makes you colder. Empty your bladder before settling in.
  6. Hot water bottle: fill a leak-proof bottle made for hot liquids with hot water, close it tightly, check for leaks, wrap it in a sock or cloth and put it in your sleeping bag by your core or thighs. Never against bare skin — it can burn.
  7. Share heat: sleep close together; put the coldest person in the middle.
  8. Vapour barrier (deep cold only): a plastic bag or liner inside your insulation, over a thin base layer, stops body moisture soaking your bag on multi-day trips well below freezing. It feels clammy — use it briefly and only when it's very cold.

Spot and treat hypothermia: see First aid.

Knots

Five knots cover most camp jobs. In the diagrams the coloured rope is the working end (the end you move) and the plain rope is the standing part (the rest of the rope). A gap in a line means that strand passes underneath.

Not for life support without training

Climbing, rescue, lowering people or anything where a failure could kill needs proper instruction, rated rope and hardware. Practise every knot until you can tie it with your eyes closed — and with cold hands.

Bowline a fixed loop that won't slip or jam

Use it for: a loop round a tree or post (one end of a ridgeline), a loop to hang or haul things. Easy to untie even after a heavy load.

  1. Leave enough rope for the loop size you want. Make a small loop (the "hole") in the standing part, with the part leading to the working end lying on top.
  2. "The rabbit comes up out of the hole": pass the working end up through the hole from underneath.
  3. "Round the tree": take it round behind the standing part.
  4. "Back down the hole": pass it back down through the hole, alongside where it came up.
  5. Hold the tail and the loop side together and pull the standing part to tighten. Leave a long tail — at least a hand-width.

Check: the tail ends up inside the big loop; a neat collar wraps the standing part; the small loop grips both strands. If the tail is outside the loop or it pulls into a slip knot, untie and retie.

Limitations

A bowline can shake loose when it isn't loaded (flapping guy lines, stiff or slippery rope), and it can't be tied or untied under load. For anything important, back it up: tie a stopper knot (double overhand) with the tail around the loop leg next to it, or use a Yosemite finish (tail round that loop leg and back up through the collar beside the standing part).

Taut-line hitch adjustable tension for guy lines and ridgelines

Use it for: tent and tarp guy lines, a ridgeline end. Slide it to tighten or loosen; it grips under load.

  1. Pass the working end around the stake, peg or tree and back toward the load, alongside the standing part.
  2. Wrap it twice around the standing part, working back toward the stake — these two turns are inside the loop.
  3. Bring the end back out past those turns, to the side away from the stake (outside the loop).
  4. Make one more turn around the standing part in the same direction, and tuck the end under itself (a half hitch). Pull snug.
  5. Slide the knot along the standing part to set the tension. Let go: it should hold.

Check: two turns between the outside hitch and the stake, one hitch outside, all turns going the same way round. It should slide only when you push the knot itself.

Limitations

Slips on slippery, stiff, very thin or icy cord — add a third inside turn. Re-check tension after rain or a temperature change.

Clove hitch quick attachment to a post, pole or tree

Use it for: tying a line to a pole or stake quickly, starting and finishing lashings, adjusting a line midway along a rope.

  1. Pass the working end around the post.
  2. Cross over the standing part — this diagonal is the rider — and go around the post a second time, in the same direction.
  3. Tuck the end under the rider, so it comes out between the two turns.
  4. Pull both ends tight.

Check: from the front, one diagonal rider with both ends coming out from under it, in opposite directions. From the back, two neat parallel turns.

Limitations

A clove hitch can slip or roll on its own — when the pull comes from the side, the pole turns, or the load keeps shifting. Secure it with two half hitches of the tail around the standing part.

Square (reef) knot bandages, bundles, tying the two ends of one cord

Use it for: tying off a bandage or sling, closing a bundle or bag — the two ends of the same cord around something. Lies flat; easy to untie.

  1. Right over left and tuck under (like starting to tie a shoe).
  2. Left over right and tuck under.
  3. Pull both ends evenly. Each end lies beside its own standing part.

Check: it lies flat and symmetric, with both short ends on the same side. If you did right-over-left twice you get a granny knot: it looks twisted, the ends stick out crosswise, and it slips or jams — retie it.

Not for joining ropes

Never use a square knot to join two ropes — especially of different size or material — or for any load that matters. It can capsize and pull apart. To join two ropes use a sheet bend (below).

Prusik friction hitch that grips when loaded and slides when not

Use it for: a movable attachment point on a ridgeline (e.g. tensioning a tarp), a grip to pull on a rope, a hauling or backup grab. You need a closed loop of cord thinner than the main rope — typically 5–7 mm cord on 10–11 mm rope.

  1. Hold the cord loop behind the main rope.
  2. Pass the far end of the loop over the rope and through itself (a girth hitch), but don't tighten.
  3. Keep wrapping through the inside of the loop, each wrap beside the last, for 3 wraps in total.
  4. Dress it: wraps neat and parallel with no crossovers, a straight bridge on one side, legs coming out of the middle.
  5. Pull the legs to test: it should grip. To move it, unload it and push the knot body, not the legs.

Check: six tidy coils, legs centred, grips firmly when you hang on it. If it slips, add a wrap or use thinner cord; if it won't slide, take a wrap off.

Limitations

Grips poorly on icy, wet, muddy or slick rope, and with cord too close to the main rope's thickness. A sudden shock can make it slip and melt the cord. Using a prusik to hold a person (climbing, ascending, rescue) requires training and rated gear.

Also useful sheet bend, trucker's hitch, two half hitches, figure-eight stopper
  • Sheet bend — joins two ropes, even of different thickness. Make a U-shaped bight in the thicker rope. Pass the thinner end up through the bight, around behind both legs of the bight, then tuck it under itself (not under the bight). Both short ends should end up on the same side. Go around twice (double sheet bend) for slippery or very unequal ropes. Leave long tails.
  • Trucker's hitch — high tension. Tie a fixed loop partway along the standing part (e.g. a figure-eight on a bight). Take the end round the anchor, back up through that loop, and pull down — it multiplies your pull (less in practice, because of friction). Pinch it and finish with two half hitches. Friction wears the rope where it runs through the loop.
  • Two half hitches — tie a rope end to a ring, post or tree. Pass the end around the object, then around the standing part and through (one half hitch); repeat in the same direction. Start with a full extra turn around the object (round turn and two half hitches) to take a heavy load.
  • Figure-eight stopper — stops a rope end pulling through. Make a loop near the end, take the end around behind the standing part, then down through the loop. It looks like an 8, holds well and unties easily.

The quiet killers

Carbon monoxide (CO)

Never run a stove, heater, grill or generator inside a tent, snow shelter, vehicle or home: carbon monoxide kills. You can't see or smell it. A generator must be at least 20 ft (6 m) from doors and windows, exhaust pointed away.

Warning signs: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion, sleepiness — often several people at once. Get everyone into fresh air immediately and get medical help. Stuck in a vehicle in snow? Keep the exhaust pipe clear of snow.

Wildfire

Most wildfires are started by people. Clear the site, keep fires small, never leave one unattended, and put it out cold. Skip fire entirely in high wind or very dry conditions.

If a fire gets away or a wildfire approaches: leave early. Fire moves fastest uphill and downwind — don't try to outrun it uphill. Head for natural firebreaks: wide rocky ground, water, roads or already-burned ground. Avoid narrow canyons and gullies, which act like chimneys.

Hypothermia from wet clothing

Wet clothes plus wind can make you dangerously cold even well above freezing. Watch for shivering, clumsiness, slurred speech, poor decisions — shivering that stops is a bad sign.

Handle the person gently. Get them out of wind and wet, remove wet clothing, insulate them from the ground, and warm the core (armpits, chest, groin). Warm sweet drinks only if fully alert and able to swallow. No alcohol. Don't rub limbs. More in First aid.
