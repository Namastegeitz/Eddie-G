Finding & Purifying Water

Water comes before food: you can last only about 3 days without it. Find the cleanest source you can, clear it, kill the germs, and learn to recognise water that no home method can make safe.

For emergencies and learning. These methods lower the risk; none is perfect. Get medical help for severe diarrhoea, vomiting, or signs of severe dehydration.

Quick reference

  1. Drink what you need. Don't ration water. Plan on at least 1 gal (3.8 L) per person per day, and more in heat or hard work. Ration sweat, not water.
  2. Pick the cleanest source: rain or spring, then fast-moving stream, then lake, then stagnant water. Never use floodwater, water with a fuel sheen or chemical smell, or water under algae scum.
  3. Clear it first: let it settle, then pour it through folded cloth or a filter.
  4. Kill the germs: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m), or unscented bleach (6%: 2 drops per litre, 8 drops per gallon) and wait 30 minutes, or SODIS for 6 hours in full sun.
  5. Store it clean and covered in labelled, food-grade containers. Keep dirty and clean containers apart.
Boil if in doubt

Unsure about a source, a filter or a method? Boil it. A rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes above 6,500 ft / 2,000 m) kills bacteria, viruses and parasites, including Cryptosporidium, which bleach misses.

Boiling does not remove chemicals. If you suspect fuel, metals, pesticides or toxic algae, find another source.

How much water you need

SituationPer person, per day
Drinking only, mild weather, restingAbout 3–4 L (3–4 qt) minimum
Heat, hard work, illness, pregnancy or breastfeedingMuch more, often double
Planning figure (drinking + basic hygiene)1 gal (3.8 L). That barely covers drinking in mild weather, so store more if you can.
How much to store at homeAt least 3 days' worth; 2 weeks is better. Add extra for pets.
  • Don't ration water. Drink what you need today and look for more tomorrow. Rationing leads to collapse sooner.
  • Ration sweat instead. Work in the cool of morning and evening. Rest in shade. Keep skin covered. Don't drink alcohol. If water is short, eat less, especially salty and high-protein food, because digesting it uses water.
  • Never drink seawater, urine or alcohol. They make dehydration worse.
  • Aim for pale straw-coloured urine.

Dehydration signs

StageSignsDo this
EarlyThirst, dark yellow urine, peeing less, dry mouth, headache, tiredness, irrit­abilityDrink now: small sips, often. Rest in shade.
SeriousDizziness, very little urine, fast heartbeat, cramps, sunken eyesStop work. Rehydrate with oral rehydration solution (below).
EmergencyConfusion, fainting, no urine for many hours, very fast breathing or heartbeat. Babies: dry nappies (diapers) for hours, no tears, sunken soft spot on the headGet medical help. Give sips only if they're fully awake and can swallow.
Oral rehydration solution (ORS)

Use ORS packets if you have them. Home recipe (WHO): 1 litre of safe water + 6 level teaspoons of sugar + ½ level teaspoon of salt. Stir until dissolved and sip. Measure carefully, because too much salt is harmful. Useful after diarrhoea, vomiting or heavy sweating. For heat illness see First aid.

Finding water

Think like water: it runs downhill and collects in low, shady places. Save your strength. Wandering in the heat costs more water than it finds.

Water already in your home water heater, pipes, ice
  • Water heater tank (often 30–80 gal / 110–300 L): turn off the gas or power first. Close the cold-water inlet valve so no contaminated water gets in. Open a hot tap somewhere to let air in, then drain from the valve at the bottom. Let the sediment settle. Careful: it may be scalding hot.
  • Pipes: close the main water valve, open the highest tap in the house, then drain from the lowest tap.
  • Ice cubes, and the liquid in canned fruit and vegetables.
  • If there was a boil-water notice before you closed the supply, treat this water too.
  • Not for drinking: heating radiators and boilers, waterbeds, swimming pools and hot tubs (use them for washing and flushing only).
Reading the land clues that point to water
  • Low ground: valley floors, gullies, the foot of cliffs and rock outcrops, shaded hollows.
  • Green lines in dry country: willows, cottonwoods, sycamores, reeds, rushes and cattails grow where water is at or near the surface.
  • Insects: mosquitoes and flies stay near water. Bees fly regularly to and from water.
  • Birds at dawn and dusk: seed-eating birds (doves, pigeons, finches) often fly low and direct towards water. Birds of prey are no guide.
  • Animal trails that converge and lead downhill often end at water.
  • Rock: after rain, look for water held in hollows and cracks in rock.
  • Listen for running water, and for frogs at night.

These are clues, not guarantees.

Springs often the best natural source
  • Look at the base of slopes and where rock layers meet the ground, often marked by a patch of lush green.
  • Collect right where the water comes out of the ground, before animals or surface run-off can foul it.
  • Dig out a small pool, let it clear, and dip water gently from the top.
  • Still treat it. Clear and cold doesn't mean safe.
Dig a seep in a dry stream bed outside of the bend, lowest point
  1. Pick the spot: the lowest point on the outside of a bend in a dry stream bed. Look for damp sand or water-loving plants.
  2. Dig in the cool of the day, about 30–60 cm (1–2 ft) down, or until the sand turns damp and then a little deeper. If it's still bone-dry after a short dig, try another spot rather than sweat away water.
  3. Wait. Water may take minutes to hours to seep in.
  4. Let the silt settle, then scoop gently from the top and strain through cloth.
  5. Disinfect it (boil or chemicals).

On a beach: dig behind the first dune, well above the high-tide line. Fresh water can collect on top of salt water underground. Taste a small sip, and if it's salty, don't drink it. Disinfect before drinking.

Dew collection before sunrise, with cloth
  1. Before dawn, tie clean absorbent cloth (cotton or wool) around your shins and walk through tall grass. Or wipe a cloth over grass, leaves, a tarp or a car body.
  2. Wring it into a container. Repeat until the dew is gone. That happens soon after sunrise.
  3. Disinfect before drinking.

Worth doing on heavy-dew mornings, but the yield is often small. Avoid poisonous plants, sprayed crops and roadside verges.

Transpiration bags clear bag over a leafy branch; small yield
  1. Choose a sunny, leafy branch of a plant you know is non-toxic. Never use poisonous plants such as oleander, yew, or poison ivy, oak or sumac. If you can't identify it, don't use it.
  2. Slip a clear plastic bag over the branch. Put a small pebble in one corner to make a low point.
  3. Tie the open end tightly around the branch.
  4. Leave it in full sun. Water collects in the low corner.
  5. Drain it by untying or making a small cut, then retie. Move the bag to fresh leaves each day.

Yield is small: often a cupful or less per bag per day, so use several bags. Discard bitter or odd-tasting water.

Snow and ice melt first, never eat it when cold
  • Never eat snow or ice when you're cold. It steals body heat and pushes you towards hypothermia. Melt it first.
  • Prefer ice to snow. Snow is mostly air, so it takes many potfuls (and lots of fuel) to make one pot of water. Ice gives far more water per pot.
  • Melting snow in a pot: start with a little water in the bottom, then add snow a bit at a time. This keeps the pot from scorching.
  • Without fire: pack snow in a bottle and carry it inside your jacket, between layers (not against bare skin).
  • Use clean white snow away from roads, camps and animal areas.
  • Treat the meltwater like any surface water. Freezing doesn't kill germs.
Rain capture tarp, roof, gutters, first flush

1 mm of rain on 1 m² = 1 litre
1 in of rain on 1,000 sq ft ≈ 620 gal

Measure the roof's footprint (its area seen from above), not its slope. You'll catch somewhat less because of splash and the first flush.

  1. Tarp: tie it at the corners with one low point over a clean container. Let the first rain rinse it. Avoid tarps that smell of chemicals.
  2. Roof: clean the gutters and screen them against leaves. Let the first few minutes of rain wash the roof before collecting, or fit a first-flush diverter that dumps the dirtiest first water (dust, bird droppings).
  3. Collect into covered containers so mosquitoes can't breed and debris can't fall in.
  4. Treat roof water before drinking. Rain is clean when it falls, but it picks up germs and dirt from the roof.
Roof cautions

Best: painted or coated metal, clay or concrete tile, a clean tarp.

Use for washing only if you have a better drinking source: asphalt shingles, treated wood shakes, old asbestos-cement sheets, copper roofs or gutters, roofs with lead flashing or old lead paint, and roofs treated with moss killer. Heavy smoke, ash or industrial air pollution can also contaminate rainwater.

Treatment overview: clear, disinfect, store

  1. Clear it. Let it settle, pour off the top, and strain through cloth or a filter. Cloudy water shields germs from chemicals and sunlight. How to.
  2. Disinfect it. Boil (best), use chemicals, or use SODIS. A filter rated for bacteria and protozoa plus a chemical covers viruses too.
  3. Store it clean. Covered, labelled, in a clean container. How to.

Boiling: the most reliable method

  1. If it's cloudy, settle it and strain it through cloth first.
  2. Bring it to a rolling boil: big bubbles that keep coming even when you stir.
  3. Keep it rolling for 1 minute. Above 6,500 ft (2,000 m), boil for 3 minutes.
  4. Let it cool, covered. Don't add ice unless the ice was made from safe water.
  5. Store it covered in a clean container.
  6. Flat taste? Pour it back and forth between two clean containers, or add a pinch of salt per litre.
  • Boiling longer than this wastes fuel and doesn't make it safer. A lid gets it to the boil faster.
  • Boiling does not remove salt, fuel, metals or other chemicals.
  • No pot? Any clean metal container or can will do. (See Fire & shelter for making fire.)

Chemical treatment

Use the right bleach

Use plain, unscented household bleach (sodium hypochlorite). Check the strength on the label: 6% or 8.25%. Do not use scented, "splash-less", colour-safe, or bleach with added cleaners. Bleach weakens with age and heat, so use a fresh bottle if you can.

For bigger containers, multiply the per-gallon dose by the number of gallons.

Bleach, step by step

  1. Settle and filter cloudy water first.
  2. Add the drops from the table with a clean dropper. Double them for cloudy, coloured or very cold water.
  3. Stir or shake well.
  4. Wait at least 30 minutes.
  5. Smell it. It should have a slight chlorine smell. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.
  6. Too strong? Pour it back and forth between two clean containers to air it.
Know the limits
  • Bleach does NOT reliably kill Cryptosporidium. Boiling does. Chlorine dioxide can, but needs up to 4 hours.
  • Iodine: not for pregnancy, thyroid problems, iodine allergy, or long-term use (more than a few weeks). Follow the product label. It doesn't kill Cryptosporidium.
  • No chemical removes fuel, metals, pesticides or algae toxins.

SODIS: solar disinfection

Ultraviolet light and heat from the sun kill most germs in clear water in clear bottles. It's free and needs no fuel, but you need clear water and strong sun.

  1. Bottle: clear PET plastic (recycling code 1, "PET" or "PETE"), 2 L or smaller. Remove the labels. Replace bottles that are badly scratched or cloudy.
  2. Water must be clear. Stand the filled bottle on a newspaper headline and look down through the opening. If you can't read the headline, settle or filter the water first.
  3. Fill and cap the bottle.
  4. Lay the bottles flat in full sun, ideally on a metal roof or sheet. Make sure nothing will shade them during the day.
  5. Leave for 6 hours of full sun. If more than half the sky is cloudy, leave for 2 consecutive days.
  6. Drink straight from the bottle or a clean cup. Don't pour it into a dirty container.
SODIS limits

Don't use it on rainy days. It does nothing for chemicals. It doesn't work in coloured glass or in big containers. When in doubt, boil.

Filtering: settling, commercial filters, improvised filters

Settle and pre-filter (always worth doing)

  1. Let cloudy water stand in a bucket until the sediment sinks: 30 minutes for slightly cloudy water, overnight for muddy water.
  2. Pour or siphon the clear top water into another container without stirring up the bottom.
  3. Pour it through folded clean cloth: several layers of cotton such as a T-shirt or bandana, or a coffee filter.
  4. Now disinfect it. Clear water lets boiling, chemicals and sunlight work properly.

Commercial filters

  • Hollow-fibre filters (0.1–0.2 micron) remove bacteria and protozoa (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) but not viruses.
  • Water that may carry sewage (near towns, floodwater, farm run-off, countries with poor sanitation) also needs chemical treatment or boiling after filtering. Filters labelled as purifiers are rated to handle viruses as well.
  • Keep them from freezing. A wet filter that freezes can crack with no visible sign. Sleep with it in your sleeping bag in cold weather. If it may have frozen, treat its output with chemicals or boil it, or replace the filter.
  • Keep the dirty and clean sides apart. Backflush as the maker says. Pre-filter muddy water so it doesn't clog.
  • A carbon stage improves taste, but don't rely on it for fuel, metals or algae toxins.

Improvised sand and charcoal filter

It does not make water safe

An improvised sand/charcoal filter improves clarity and taste only. It does NOT make water safe. Always disinfect afterwards (boil, chemicals or SODIS).

Build it, step by step bottle or bucket
  1. Container: a large plastic bottle with the bottom cut off, hung upside down with the cap removed. Or a bucket with a small hole near the bottom.
  2. Plug the neck or hole with clean cloth or cotton.
  3. Make charcoal from your own fire: black, burned-through pieces of untreated wood (not ash, not painted or treated wood, not barbecue briquettes, which contain additives). Crush to pea-size or smaller and rinse out the dust.
  4. Rinse the sand and gravel until the rinse water runs fairly clear.
  5. Add layers from the bottom up: gravel, coarse sand, crushed charcoal, fine sand, then cloth on top. In a bucket, roughly: gravel 5 cm (2 in), coarse sand 5 cm (2 in), charcoal 5–8 cm (2–3 in), fine sand 10–15 cm (4–6 in). In a bottle, scale down but keep fine sand the thickest layer, and leave room at the top for water.
  6. Pour water in slowly so you don't dig holes in the sand.
  7. Throw away the first few runs until what comes out is clear.
  8. Collect into a clean container and DISINFECT IT by boiling, chemicals or SODIS.
  9. Rinse or replace the sand and charcoal when flow slows or the water stops clearing. Germs build up in it.

Solar still

The sun evaporates moisture under a plastic sheet. It condenses on the underside and drips into a container. The condensed water is distilled, so it's free of salt and usually free of germs.

Honest yield

Realistic yield is small, often a few hundred mL per day per pit. It is not a primary water source. In heat you can sweat out more digging the pit than it gives back, so dig in the cool of the day.

Build it, step by step pit, container, sheet, stone
  1. Pick a sunny spot with damp-looking soil if possible, such as a low area or a dry stream bed.
  2. Dig a bowl-shaped pit about 1 m (3 ft) across and 60 cm (2 ft) deep.
  3. Set a container in the centre at the bottom. Add a drinking tube running from it out of the pit if you have one.
  4. Add moisture around (not into) the container: chopped green non-toxic plants, or salt water, urine or dirty water poured into the soil.
  5. Cover the pit with clear plastic. Seal the edges all round with soil and rocks so no vapour escapes.
  6. Put a small stone in the middle of the sheet so it sags into a cone, with the low point directly over the container but not touching it.
  7. Leave it. Drink through the tube so you don't open the still and lose the trapped moisture.

Don't put fuel-contaminated water in a still. Some fuels evaporate and condense along with the water.

When purification isn't enough

No home method fixes chemical contamination

No home method removes fuel, heavy metals, pesticides, industrial chemicals or toxic algae toxins. Boiling can concentrate some of them. Find another source.

Red flags: walk away

  • Fuel or oil sheen, a rainbow film on the surface.
  • Chemical, fuel or solvent smell, or unusual foam.
  • Dead fish or dead animals in or near the water.
  • Mine drainage and tailings: orange, red or white-stained rocks, unnaturally clear or oddly coloured water, piles of crushed rock nearby.
  • Agricultural run-off: below fields, feedlots and orchards (pesticides, fertiliser, manure).
  • Below industry, dumps, roads after a spill, or wrecked vehicles.
  • Floodwater: presume it's contaminated with sewage, fuel and chemicals. Don't drink it or wash dishes in it. Wash your skin after contact.
  • Blue-green algae scum: water that looks like pea soup or spilled paint, green or blue-green streaks, or mats. Its toxins survive boiling. Keep pets and livestock away, because it can kill dogs quickly. Don't use it for cooking or bathing either.

Instead: collect rain, find a spring, go upstream of the problem, or use stored water.

Storing treated water

  1. Use food-grade containers: water jugs or barrels made for drinking water, or PET drink bottles. Never use containers that held chemicals, fuel or non-food products. Avoid milk and juice jugs, which are hard to clean and break down.
  2. Wash with soap and water, then rinse.
  3. Sanitize: mix 1 teaspoon (5 mL) of unscented bleach in 1 quart (about 1 L) of water. Pour it in, cap, and shake so it touches every inside surface. Wait at least 30 seconds, pour it out, then air-dry or rinse with safe water.
  4. Fill with safe water (treated, or from a safe tap). Don't touch the inside of the cap or neck.
  5. Label it "Drinking water" with the date.
  6. Store it cool and dark, away from fuel, pesticides and chemicals, whose vapours can pass through plastic.
  7. Rotate it about every 6 months. Use the old water for washing or cooking and refill.
  8. Pour, don't dip. Use a tap or a clean scoop. Never put hands or used cups into stored water.

Backcountry and travel notes

  • Carry two methods, such as a filter plus tablets, or a stove plus tablets, in case one fails, freezes or runs out.
  • Collect upstream of trails, camps, livestock and toilets.
  • Bury human waste at least 200 ft (60 m) from water in a hole 6–8 in (15–20 cm) deep.
  • Mark "dirty" and "clean" bottles. Don't let untreated water drip onto drinking threads or caps.
  • Wash or sanitize your hands before eating and after the toilet. Many "water" illnesses actually come from hands.
  • Cold weather: keep filters from freezing (sleep with them). Store bottles upside-down so ice forms at the bottom, not in the lid. Chemicals work slower in cold water, so give them longer and double the bleach dose.
  • Where tap water isn't safe: use sealed bottled water or treated water for drinking, brushing teeth and ice. Hot drinks made with boiled water are fine.
  • Plan your carry: know where the next reliable water is before you leave the last one.
